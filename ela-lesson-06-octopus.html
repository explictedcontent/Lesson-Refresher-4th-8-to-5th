Reading Passage
10 min

The Great Escape of Professor Eight

The humans call me Professor Eight. I live in a glass tank at a seaside aquarium, and I earned my name the honest way: I solve every puzzle the staff gives me. Locked boxes, twisting tubes, mazes with hidden treats — I open them all. They cheer and clap. I just wave an arm. For a professor, it's all in a day's work.

Humans think we octopuses aren't watching, but we are. I can distinguish every person who walks past my tank. There is the kind feeder with the gentle hands who brings me crabs. And there is the one who taps on my glass — tap, tap, tap — as if I were a television. I squirt water at that one. He deserves it.

At night, when the visitors go home and the lights hum low, my real work begins. I investigate every inch of my tank: every rock, every corner, every bubbling tube. My flexible arms reach up and test the lid, night after night. My curiosity never sleeps, even when I pretend to.

The staff once left a glass jar in my tank with a live crab sealed inside. A screw-top lid! Did they think that would stop me? I wrapped my arms around it, twisted, and — pop — dinner was served. I didn't even need my camouflage to sneak up on it.

Then one night, everything changed. A maintenance worker, in a hurry to go home, left my tank lid slightly loose. Just a crack. But a crack is a doorway if you have no bones.

I formed my strategy at once: wait until the last light went out, squeeze through the gap, and slide down the outside of the tank. Simple. When darkness finally settled, I pushed one arm through the opening, then another, then squeezed my whole soft body — no bones, remember — through a space barely wider than my eye. Down the cold glass I slid, landing on the floor with a wet plop.

Now came the dangerous part. An octopus can only survive a few minutes out of water, so I had to move fast. I pulled myself across the damp floor, arm over arm, following a smell that called to me like a song: the smell of the sea, drifting up from a drain in the floor.

I poured myself into the drain pipe and let it carry me down, down, down — until suddenly the pipe ended and the whole ocean opened around me. Cold, dark, endless. Freedom.

Sometimes I wonder if the humans tell stories about my remarkable escape. They should. After all, it really happened — a real octopus named Inky escaped New Zealand's National Aquarium through a drain pipe in 2016. Curiosity, it turns out, opens more doors than any key.

1. Why do the aquarium workers call her "Professor Eight"? ▶

2. How can Professor Eight fit through the small gap in the lid? ▶
Octopuses have no bones — they can squeeze their soft bodies through any gap bigger than their beak.
3. What is Professor Eight's strategy for escaping? ▶
Wait for darkness, squeeze through the loose lid, cross the floor, and follow the smell of the sea into the drain pipe.
4. Why must she cross the floor quickly? ▶
Octopuses can only survive out of water for a few minutes — if she moved too slowly, she wouldn't make it to the drain.
5. What true story inspired this passage? ▶
Inky the octopus, who escaped New Zealand's National Aquarium through a drain pipe in 2016.
