Core Reading: Sea Turtles Around the World

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

Sea turtles have swum Earth's oceans for more than 100 million years — they shared the planet with dinosaurs! Today, seven species survive worldwide: the Green, Loggerhead, Leatherback, Hawksbill, Kemp's Ridley, Flatback, and Olive Ridley. Each species has a unique look and lifestyle, but all face serious threats from human activity.

The Leatherback is the largest sea turtle — it can weigh over 1,000 pounds and reach 7 feet long. Its name comes from its rubbery shell, which lacks hard scutes. Leatherbacks are champion travelers: one individual tracked by scientists swam more than 10,000 miles round trip from Canada all the way to Trinidad. They also cross the entire Pacific Ocean, migrating from Asia to California and back.

Not all sea turtles migrate so far, but they all return to the beach where they were born to lay eggs — sometimes after 20 or 30 years away. The most famous nesting place in the world is Tortuguero, Costa Rica, where tens of thousands of Green turtles come ashore each year. Sometimes hundreds of sea turtles nest on the same beach on the same night, creating a spectacular event called an "arribada" (Spanish for "arrival"). The world's largest Green turtle rookery is Raine Island, Australia, where over 60,000 turtles can nest in a single season. Other key nesting sites include Archie Carr National Wildlife Refuge in Florida, and important beaches in Malaysia and Japan.

Rookery: A breeding colony — a place where animals gather to nest and raise young.

Protecting sea turtles requires cooperation between countries because these animals travel across international borders. In 1973, world governments created CITES — the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species. Today, 183 countries have signed this treaty, agreeing not to buy or sell sea turtles or their products.

The IUCN Red List tracks how endangered each species is. Right now, the Kemp's Ridley and Hawksbill are Critically Endangered. The Green turtle is Endangered. The Leatherback and Loggerhead are Vulnerable.

The Kemp's Ridley is the rarest sea turtle. In 1985, scientists counted only about 200 nests on the entire planet — the species was nearly extinct. Conservationists rallied: they protected nesting beaches in Mexico, required shrimp boats to use special escape hatches called Turtle Excluder Devices (TEDs), and moved eggs to protected hatcheries. By 2019, the count had risen to over 9,000 nests per year — one of the greatest sea turtle recovery stories ever recorded.

The oldest marine turtle conservation organization in the world is the Sea Turtle Conservancy, founded in 1959 by Archie Carr in Florida. It pioneered tagging and tracking programs that taught us how far these animals travel.

Despite the progress, sea turtles still face many threats. Beach development destroys nesting habitat and bright lights confuse hatchlings that use moonlight to find the ocean. Plastic bags floating in the water look exactly like jellyfish — a favorite food — and turtles that eat them can choke or starve. Fishing bycatch accidentally catches turtles in nets and longlines. And climate change is warming beaches, which affects the sex of hatchlings — warmer sand produces more females, which could eventually skew the population.
