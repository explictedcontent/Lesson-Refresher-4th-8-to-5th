👋 For the Grown-Up / Teacher

Lesson 3 shifts from stories to nonfiction — a key 5th-grade skill. The learner becomes a Fact Finder who digs out the main idea, gathers key details, and boils a whole article down to a short summary. Read the orange "SAY THIS" lines aloud and let them explain their thinking. Type into boxes on screen or print it. Answer key is at the bottom.

You'll need: pencil/scratch paper and a highlighter (or colored pencil) if printing.

📰 Part 2 — Read the Article 12 min

"Read like a Fact Finder. Each section has a heading — that's a clue to the main idea!"

Octopuses: The Ocean's Clever Escape Artists

The octopus may be one of the smartest animals in the entire ocean. These soft-bodied creatures have amazed scientists with their surprising brainpower and their clever tricks for staying safe.

Master of Disguise

One of the octopus's best skills is camouflage. In less than a second, it can change the color AND the texture of its skin to match a rock, a patch of sand, or a bed of coral. This helps it hide from hungry predators and sneak up on its own prey. Some octopuses can even copy the shape of other sea animals, like a flat, poisonous fish, to scare enemies away.

Brainy Problem-Solvers

Octopuses are excellent problem-solvers. In laboratories, they have learned to open jars to reach the food inside, and they can find their way through tricky mazes. They are also famous escape artists. Because an octopus has no bones, it can squeeze its entire body through a hole no bigger than its eyeball — which means a small gap in a tank is all it needs to slip away.

Built for Survival

If hiding and squeezing don't work, the octopus has a backup plan. It can shoot a cloud of dark ink into the water to confuse a predator while it jets away to safety. With three hearts pumping blue blood and eight powerful arms, the octopus is built to survive in a dangerous ocean.

📌 Tip: read it twice. The first time for fun, the second time hunting for facts.

Part 6 — Writing: Use the checklist. A strong summary names the topic, states the main idea in the student's own words, includes 2–3 key details, stays to 3–4 sentences, and leaves out opinions/tiny facts. Praise précis over copying sentences word-for-word.
