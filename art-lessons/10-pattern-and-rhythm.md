# Lesson 10: Pattern & Rhythm

**Principles of Design:** Pattern, Rhythm, Repetition
**Time:** 30 minutes

## Learning goal
The student can create a repeating pattern and use rhythm to lead the viewer's eye
across an artwork.

## Vocabulary
- **Pattern** — a design that repeats over and over.
- **Motif** — the single unit that repeats (one shape, one image).
- **Repetition** — using the same element again and again.
- **Rhythm** — the visual "beat" created by repeating elements; it can be regular (steady)
  or flowing (changing).
- **Alternating pattern** — two motifs that take turns (●▲●▲●▲).

## Materials
- White paper
- Markers, crayons, or colored pencils
- Ruler (optional, for grids)

## Lesson plan (30 minutes)

**Warm-Up — 5 min**
Make a simple repeating border around the edge of your paper using one motif (dots, then
the same dot again and again). Then try an alternating border (dot, star, dot, star).

**Concept / Mini-Lesson — 7 min**
Patterns are everywhere — clothes, wallpaper, animal skins, brick walls. **Rhythm** is
like music for your eyes: a steady beat (same motif, even spacing) feels calm; a flowing
beat (motifs that grow, shrink, or curve) feels lively and moves your eye around. Point
out rhythm in something nearby (tiles, fence posts, a zebra).

**Guided Practice — 6 min**
Pick one motif (leaf, fish, star). Draw it 5 times in a row keeping it the same size =
**regular rhythm**. Then draw it 5 more times getting bigger each time = **flowing/
progressive rhythm**. Compare how each feels.

**Main Activity — 10 min: "Patterned Object"**
Draw a large simple object (a shoe, a vase, a fish, a hot-air balloon) and fill it
completely with patterns — different patterns in different sections. This is in the spirit
of "Zentangle" / pattern-filled art.

**Reflection / Share — 2 min**
Which pattern has the strongest rhythm? Does your eye move around the artwork?

## If You Finish Early (+10–15 min)
**Tessellation Starter:** On graph or grid paper, repeat one shape so the copies fit
together with no gaps (squares, triangles, or hexagons). Color them in an alternating
pattern. This is how artists like M.C. Escher built interlocking designs.

## What to look for (teacher check)
- A clear, consistent repeating motif.
- Student can describe the difference between regular and flowing rhythm.
