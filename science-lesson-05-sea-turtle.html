Temperature-Dependent Sex Determination (TSD)

Most animals have chromosomes that decide whether an embryo becomes male or female. Sea turtles are different. Their sex is decided by nest temperature during incubation. If the nest stays above 29°C (84°F), more hatchlings will be female. Below 29°C, more will be male. At exactly 29°C — called the "pivotal temperature" — roughly equal numbers of males and females hatch.

This creates a serious problem with climate change. As global temperatures rise, beach sand heats up, pushing nest temperatures higher. Scientists monitoring Florida beaches have found ratios as extreme as 99% female in some years. Without enough males, populations cannot reproduce and will eventually collapse.

Magnetic Navigation

How does a turtle find the same tiny beach across thousands of miles of open ocean — sometimes 30 years later? The answer is magnetite crystals — tiny magnetic particles in brain cells that work like a built-in compass. These crystals detect Earth's magnetic field lines, giving turtles information about both their latitude (north-south position) and longitude (east-west position).

Every beach has a slightly different magnetic "address" in Earth's field. Female turtles memorize their birth beach's magnetic signature as hatchlings, then use that memory as adults to navigate back to nest. Researchers have confirmed turtles return to within a few miles of their exact hatch site.

Leatherback Sea Turtle: The Record-Breaker

The leatherback is the most extreme of all sea turtles. It is the only sea turtle without a hard bony shell — instead, it has thick, rubbery leathery skin over a mosaic of tiny bones. This flexible body allows it to reach depths of 3,937 feet (1,200 m) — the deepest dive of any reptile — without cracking under pressure.

Leatherbacks are also the largest reptiles by weight, reaching up to 2,000 lbs (900 kg). Unlike almost all reptiles, which are ectotherms (cold-blooded), leatherbacks can partially regulate their own body temperature through their large size and special circulation. This lets them survive in cold waters from the Arctic all the way to the tropics.

The Green Sea Turtle's Surprising Name

The green sea turtle is NOT named for the color of its shell — its shell is brownish. It is named for the green-colored fat beneath its skin. The fat turns green because green turtles are the only sea turtles that graze on plants as adults, eating large amounts of seagrass and algae. The chlorophyll in these plants tints their fat green.

Phototaxis: Following the Light

Hatchlings are born with a behavior called phototaxis — they automatically move toward the brightest light they can see. In nature, that's the reflection of moonlight and starlight on the ocean surface. But on developed beaches, streetlights, parking lots, and houses are brighter. Disoriented hatchlings crawl toward roads and lights instead of the sea, where they die from dehydration, predators, or cars.

Breathing and Diving

Sea turtles are air-breathing reptiles — they must surface to breathe. But they can hold their breath for impressive lengths of time: up to 7 hours while resting on the sea floor (their heart slows to just one beat per minute!), and about 45 minutes while actively swimming.

Ancient Survivors

Sea turtles have roamed Earth's oceans for over 100 million years — they watched dinosaurs come and go. They survived the asteroid impact that wiped out most life on Earth 66 million years ago. Today, six of the seven sea turtle species are threatened or endangered, mostly due to human activity: plastic pollution, fishing nets, coastal development, and climate change.
