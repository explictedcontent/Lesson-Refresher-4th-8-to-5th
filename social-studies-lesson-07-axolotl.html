Core Reading: The Water Monster of Mexico City

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

The word axolotl means "water monster" in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs. But this "monster" is a smiling, feathery-gilled salamander that never grows up! According to Aztec legend, the god Xolotl — the god of fire and lightning — was about to be sacrificed. To escape, he transformed himself into an axolotl and hid in the water. That legend was how the Aztecs explained this strange, magical creature swimming in their lake.

Nahuatl: the language of the Aztecs, still spoken in Mexico today.   Chinampa: a "floating garden" farm plot built in a shallow lake.   Endangered: at risk of dying out completely.   Invasive species: an animal or plant brought to a place where it doesn't belong, harming the animals that live there.   Refuge: a safe place.

Wild axolotls live in exactly ONE place on Earth: the canals of Lake Xochimilco, on the south side of Mexico City. Seven hundred years ago, that water was part of a huge lake system — and right in the middle of it, the Aztecs built their capital city, Tenochtitlan, on islands in the lake! To feed everyone, they invented chinampas: rectangular "floating garden" farm plots built up from mud and plants in the shallow water. Chinampas were so clever that farmers in Xochimilco still use them today — the area is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

So what happened to the lake? After the Spanish conquest in 1521, the lakes were slowly drained over centuries to build modern Mexico City. Today, one of the world's biggest cities — home to over 9 million people — sits where the water used to be. Only the canals of Xochimilco remain, and that's the axolotl's last wild home.

Sadly, wild axolotls are now critically endangered. Scientists think only about 50 to 1,000 may be left in the wild. Two big problems threaten them: pollution in the canals, and invasive fish — tilapia and carp, introduced for fishing — that gobble up axolotl eggs. But there is hope! Scientists and chinampa farmers are teaming up to build axolotl refuges: cleaned-up sections of canal with filters, where the water is safe for axolotls to live and lay eggs.

Mexicans are proud of their water monster. The axolotl is a national symbol of Mexico — it even appears on the 50-peso bill, swimming right next to a chinampa! From Aztec legend to modern money, the axolotl has been part of Mexico's story for more than 700 years.
