Built for the Deep Freeze

The Largest Penguin

The emperor penguin is the largest penguin in the world — standing up to 4 feet tall. It lives in Antarctica, the coldest place on Earth, where winter temperatures can drop below -60°F and winds scream over 100 miles per hour. Almost no other animal could survive there. The emperor penguin does more than survive — this is where it raises its chicks.

Staying Warm

An emperor penguin is a walking winter coat. It has four dense, overlapping layers of feathers — the most of any bird — and each layer traps a blanket of warm air against the skin. Under the skin is a thick layer of blubber, which is fat that holds in body heat. The feathers are even coated in oil to stay waterproof, so the penguin never gets soaked and chilled.

Warm Feet on the Ice

How do their bare feet not freeze while standing on ice all winter? They use a clever trick called counter-current heat exchange. Warm blood heading down to their feet runs right next to the cold blood coming back up. The warm blood heats the cold blood before it returns to the body. This means the penguin loses almost no heat through its feet — and its feet stay just warm enough not to freeze to the ice.

The Huddle

When the wind howls, thousands of emperor penguins pack together tightly into a giant huddle. Inside the huddle it can be surprisingly warm. The birds on the freezing outside edge slowly rotate to the warm middle, and then back out again, so everyone shares the warmth. No one has to stay in the cold too long. It is pure teamwork for survival.

Diving Champions

Emperor penguins hunt fish and squid in the ocean, and they are record-setters underwater. They dive deeper (over 1,850 feet) and longer (up to about 20 minutes) than any other bird. Their blood can store extra oxygen, letting them stay under far longer than we ever could.

The Amazing Dads

Emperor penguin fathers do one of the hardest jobs in nature. The female lays a single egg, then walks back to the sea to feed. The male balances that egg on his feet, tucked under a warm belly flap called a brood pouch, for about 65 days — through the coldest winter on Earth. The whole time he eats nothing and can lose half his body weight. When the chick finally hatches, the mother returns with food, and the parents take turns caring for it.

Flying Underwater

Emperor penguins cannot fly in the air, but they have a streamlined body and stiff flipper-like wings that let them "fly" through the water. They shoot through the ocean like torpedoes to catch fast fish. They are warm-blooded (endotherms), which means they make their own body heat and must protect it from the cold — that's why all their amazing adaptations matter.

Why Breed in Winter?

Unlike most birds, emperor penguins breed in winter. It sounds backwards, but it is smart timing: the chicks grow up and are ready to feed themselves right when summer brings the most food to the sea. Nature planned it perfectly.
