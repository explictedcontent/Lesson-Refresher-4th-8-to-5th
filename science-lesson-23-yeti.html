The DNA Test That Solved a Legend

A Hundred Years of Footprints

In the high Himalaya — the mountain wall that runs along Nepal, Tibet, India and Bhutan — people have told stories for a very long time about a large, hairy, upright creature in the snow. Western climbers picked the stories up in the early 1900s. In 1921 a British expedition reported tracks in the snow and a name they had half-understood from their guides; a newspaper writer turned it into the phrase "abominable snowman," and that phrase went around the world. In 1951 the climber Eric Shipton photographed a huge, strange footprint on a Himalayan glacier, with an ice axe laid beside it for scale. That single photograph kept the argument going for decades.

Here is something worth noticing right at the start. Several of the local names behind our word yeti — such as dzu-teh — were used by people in the mountains for a large bear. The bear was in the name the whole time. Nobody outside the mountains was listening closely enough to hear it.

For about a hundred years, that was the whole situation: stories, footprints, and arguments. What nobody had was a way to check.

What DNA Actually Is

DNA is the instruction molecule packed inside almost every cell of almost every living thing. It is a long code written with just four chemical letters — A, T, C and G — repeated in a particular order. That order is the instructions for building and running the organism.

The important part for our mystery: every species has its own distinctive DNA. A brown bear's code is not a dog's code, and a dog's is not a snow leopard's. Closely related species have codes that look nearly the same, with only small differences; distant relatives look wildly different. So if you can pull DNA out of something and compare its code to the codes of known animals, the sample tells you exactly what it came from — no guessing, no opinion.

And you barely need any of it. A single hair, a fleck of skin, a chip of bone, a scrap of a feather, a bit of droppings — each carries cells, and cells carry DNA. Scientists use this trick constantly and not just for legends: they identify a bird from one feather found under a wind turbine, catch illegal ivory by testing which elephant population it came from, and count snow leopards in a valley by testing droppings without ever seeing the animal. This method of naming a species from a short standard stretch of its code is called DNA barcoding, because it works a bit like scanning a barcode at a shop.

The Test

In 2017, a research team led by the biologist Charlotte Lindqvist did the obvious thing that nobody had properly done before. Museums, monasteries and private collectors around the world were holding relics said to be from a yeti. The team gathered 24 samples in all — hair, bone, a tooth, skin and droppings — from purported yetis and from known bears of the region. Nine of those samples were specifically claimed to be yeti.

They extracted the DNA from each one and compared the codes against the codes of known species.

What the Results Said

Eight of the nine "yeti" samples came back as bears — Himalayan brown bears, Tibetan brown bears, and Asiatic black bears, all animals that genuinely live in those mountains.

The ninth came back as a dog.

That is the whole answer, and it is worth sitting with for a second. A question people had shouted about for a century — is there a giant unknown creature up there? — was answered for these relics by lab work, calmly, in a way anyone else can repeat and check. That is what makes it evidence instead of an opinion.

The bear result also explains the eyewitnesses without calling any of them liars. A brown bear standing up on its hind legs in snow is tall, broad-shouldered, and covered in shaggy hair. Seen at a distance, in bad light, on a slope where you cannot judge size, it is a genuinely confusing sight. And footprints in snow do not stay the way they were made: as the sun works on them, the edges melt and slump outward, so a normal track slowly widens into something enormous and strange-shaped. A bear also often plants its back foot partly on top of its front footprint, which merges two tracks into one big weird one. Honest people can report exactly what they saw and still be wrong about what made it.

The Honest Limit

Now the part that separates good science from a victory lap. The 2017 study can only tell you about the samples that were tested. It proved those particular relics came from bears and a dog. It cannot prove that nothing unknown lives in the Himalaya, because you cannot test something you do not have.

Scientists say this out loud on purpose. "Every relic we tested was a known animal" is a strong, careful claim you can defend. "Nothing unknown exists anywhere in those mountains" is a much bigger claim that the evidence does not support. Knowing the difference between what your evidence shows and what you wish it showed is one of the most valuable habits a person can have.

Science also fixes itself. In 2014, an earlier study suggested that two Himalayan hair samples matched an unknown bear related to ancient polar bears — an exciting result that got a lot of headlines. Other scientists then re-examined the same data and found the match was simply a Himalayan brown bear. Nobody cheated; a result was published, checked by others, and corrected. That checking step is not a failure of science. It is science.

The Real Animals Up There

The animals the DNA pointed to are far more interesting than the legend, because every one of them is a machine built for a place that tries to kill things.

Snow leopard. A ghost-grey cat of cliffs and scree, usually between about 3,000 and 4,500 m. Its fur is thick and dense, longest on the belly. Its paws are enormous and furry underneath, spreading its weight across soft snow like a pair of snowshoes so it does not sink. Its tail is nearly as long as its body — a counterweight for balancing on ledges, and a scarf it wraps over its face when it curls up to sleep. Inside its short muzzle is an unusually large nasal cavity, which warms the freezing thin air on the way to the lungs.

Himalayan brown bear. Sandy-brown, big, and rare, living up around 3,000–5,000 m. It solves winter by not being awake for it: it fattens hard in autumn, then hibernates in a den for months, living off its own fat while food is impossible to find, insulated by that fat and by thick fur.

Asiatic black bear. Also called the moon bear for the pale crescent on its chest. It climbs well, spends summer high in Himalayan forests, and moves down to lower, warmer elevations as winter closes in — a different answer to the same problem: don't endure the cold, leave it.

Yak. A domesticated and wild ox of the Tibetan Plateau, at home from about 3,000 m to over 5,000 m. It wears a double coat: a soft woolly undercoat that traps a layer of warm air against the skin, under a long shaggy outer coat that sheds snow and wind. Inside, it has larger lungs and a larger heart than lowland cattle, and its blood carries about three times as many red blood cells, each one smaller. More red blood cells means more oxygen squeezed out of every thin-air breath. Take a yak down to the hot lowlands and it does badly — it is specialised for up there.

Bar-headed goose. Twice a year this bird crosses the Himalaya. GPS trackers have recorded one at 7,290 m, in air where a human would black out. Its trick is oxygen efficiency: a slightly different version of hemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying molecule in blood) that grabs oxygen more tightly where there is little of it, plus flight muscles packed with extra blood capillaries and extra mitochondria — the parts of a cell that turn fuel and oxygen into usable energy. It also flies mostly at night, when the air is colder and denser, and it climbs using its own muscle power.

Why High Places Are So Hard to Live In

Four problems stack up as you go higher, and every adaptation above is an answer to one of them.

1. Less oxygen. The air is not a different mixture up high — it is just spread thinner. At about 5,500 m, each breath contains roughly half the oxygen of a breath at sea level. At the summit of Mount Everest (8,849 m) it is about a third. Answers: extra red blood cells, bigger lungs and heart, tighter-gripping hemoglobin.

2. Cold. Air temperature drops roughly 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb, and mountain wind strips heat away faster still. Answers: dense double coats, fat layers, hibernation, a tail used as a scarf.

3. Strong sun. There is less atmosphere overhead to absorb ultraviolet light, and snow reflects a lot of it straight back up at you. Answers: thick fur and skin, and eyes adapted to glare.

4. Little shelter and little food. Above the treeline there are no forests to hide in and plants grow slowly and small. Answers: camouflage, huge home ranges, hibernating through the lean months, or migrating out entirely.

Think Like the Lab Did

Strip the story down and you get a pattern you can use on anything:

Claim → Evidence → Test → Conclusion (with its limits).

The claim was that an unknown creature leaves these relics. The evidence was the relics themselves, physically collected. The test was reading their DNA and comparing it to known species — a test that could have proved the claim right, which is what makes it a fair test. The conclusion was that these relics are bears and a dog, stated with its honest limit attached.

Notice the good question the scientists asked. Not "is the yeti real?" — that is nearly impossible to answer. But "what animal did these samples come from?" — that one has an answer you can actually go and get.

🔎 Myth check: A footprint is not proof of what made it, and a blurry photo is not proof either. Ask the scientist's questions: What exactly is the evidence? Could something ordinary explain it? Is there a test that could show me I'm wrong? And be fair to the people in the stories — a mistake made honestly is not a lie.
