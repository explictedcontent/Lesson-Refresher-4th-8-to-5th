📋 One Word Runs This Whole Lesson: "PER HUNDRED"

The word percent is really two words squashed together: per + cent. "Cent" means hundred — it is the same "cent" hiding inside century (100 years) and cent (100 of them make a dollar).

So percent means "per hundred," or "out of 100."

That means 45% is just a nickname for 45100. Same number, shorter outfit. The little % sign even looks like a squished 100 — a 0, a slash, and another 0.

▦ Phase 2 — The 100 Grid (12 min)

This picture is the heart of the whole lesson. Shade the squares yourself, then hit 6 targets.

📋 A Percent Is Just "How Many of the 100 Squares Are Shaded"

Take one whole thing and cut it into 100 equal little squares. Now shade some of them. However many squares you shaded — that is the percent. Nothing more clever than that.

1
Shade 45 squares → that is 45%, which is 45100 of the whole thing.
2
Shade all 100 squares → that is 100%, and 100% always means the whole thing.
3
Shade 0 squares → that is 0%, meaning none of it.
45%  =  45 out of 100  =  45100
Why 100 and not some other number? Because it makes comparing easy. If one thing is 35 and another is 710, you have to stop and think. But 60% and 70%? Instantly obvious. Percents are a common denominator that the whole world already agreed on, and that denominator is 100.
Rows are your friend on this grid. Each full row is 10 squares, so one row = 10%. Two rows = 20%. Five rows = half the grid = 50%. If you ever lose track, count in rows first and then add the leftovers one at a time.

🔁 Phase 3 — The Three Forms (15 min)

Percent, fraction and decimal are three costumes for the same number. Learn to swap between them — 6 rounds.

📋 Percent → Decimal, and Decimal → Percent

A percent is already a number over 100, and a fraction bar is a division sign. So turning a percent into a decimal just means divide by 100 — and dividing by 100 moves the decimal point two places to the LEFT.

Worked example — write 45% as a decimal
45% means 45 ÷ 100the % sign is the instruction "out of 100"
Every whole number has a hidden point on the end: 45 is really 45.
Hop it two places left: 45. → 4.5 → .45one hop, two hops
45% = 0.45
Worked example — write 0.7 as a percent
Going the other way, so multiply by 100 — the point hops two places RIGHT
0.7 → 7. → 70.there was no digit for the second hop, so a 0 fills the gap
0.7 = 70%
Careful: 0.7 is NOT 7%. 0.7 is seven tenths, and seven tenths is seventy hundredths.
% → decimal: point hops LEFT  ·  decimal → %: point hops RIGHT
Which way do I hop? Use the size test. A percent number is always bigger-looking than its decimal (45 vs 0.45). So going to a decimal, the number must get smaller → hop left. Going to a percent, it must get bigger → hop right. If your answer looks wildly wrong-sized, you hopped the wrong way.

📋 Percent → Fraction (and Back)

Put the percent number over 100, then simplify — exactly the same simplifying you already do with fractions: divide the top and the bottom by the same number until nothing but 1 fits into both.

Worked example — write 20% as a fraction in lowest terms
Over 100: 20100
Both 20 and 100 divide by 2020 ÷ 20 = 1 and 100 ÷ 20 = 5
20% = 15
Slower route: ÷10 gives 2/10, then ÷2 gives 1/5 — same place, more steps ✓

🧮 Phase 4 — Finding a Percent OF a Number (12 min)

Two friendly methods, plus the trap that catches almost every 5th grader. Then 6 practice questions.

📋 Method 1 — The 10% Trick

10% is the easiest percent in the world to find, because 10% is 110: you just divide by 10. And once you have 10%, you can build almost any other percent out of it.

Worked example — 30% of 60
First find 10%: 60 ÷ 10 = 6so 10% of 60 is 6
30% is three tens, so take 3 copies of 66 × 3 = 18
30% of 60 = 18
Check: 18 is well under 60, and 30% is well under half ✓
Worked example — 40% of 90
10% of 90 is 990 ÷ 10 = 9
40% is four tens: 9 × 4 = 36
40% of 90 = 36

📋 Method 2 — Swap It for the Friendly Fraction

If the percent is one of the Benchmark Five, forget the 10% trick and just use the fraction. You already know how to take a fraction of a number: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.

Worked example — 25% of 40
25% is 14, so this is one quarter of 40
Bottom is 4 → 40 ÷ 4 = 10
25% of 40 = 10
The 10% route works too but takes longer: 10% is 4, then 25% is two-and-a-half of those. The fraction is cleaner here.
Worked example — 75% of 20
75% is 34
Bottom is 4 → 20 ÷ 4 = 5that is one quarter
Top is 3 → 5 × 3 = 15keep three of those quarters
75% of 20 = 15
Check: the missing quarter is 5, and 15 + 5 = 20 ✓

USE THE 10% TRICK WHEN…

  • The percent ends in 0
  • 30%, 40%, 60%, 70%, 90%
  • Divide by 10, then copy it
  • 10% of 80 = 8, so 60% = 48

USE THE FRACTION WHEN…

  • It is a Benchmark Five
  • 25%, 50%, 75%
  • Divide by bottom, times top
  • 50% of 18 = 9 (just halve it)

📋 The Trap: A Bigger Percent Is NOT Always a Bigger Amount

This one catches everybody. A percent on its own is not an amount — it is a percent of something. Change what it is "of," and everything changes.

50% of 10 = 5   but   25% of 40 = 10

50% is the bigger percent, and yet it gave the smaller amount.

⚠️ Always ask "percent of WHAT?" Half of a small pile can easily be less than a quarter of a huge pile. Percents are only safe to compare directly when the whole is the same — 30% of a class beats 20% of that same class, guaranteed. But 30% of one class versus 20% of a different class? You have to work out the actual numbers.
Two anchors to sanity-check every answer. 100% of a number is the number itself — 100% of 24 is 24, unchanged. And 50% is exactly half. So if someone asks for 40% of 90 and you get 45, you can spot instantly that 45 is half of 90, so it must be 50%, not 40%. Your answer for 40% has to land a bit under half. (It is 36 ✓)
