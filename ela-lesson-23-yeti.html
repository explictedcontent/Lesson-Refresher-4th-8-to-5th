🦶 The Most Famous Footprint on Earth Tap to reveal ▾

In 1951, a British climber named Eric Shipton was crossing a glacier in the Himalaya when he found a line of huge tracks in the snow. He laid his ice axe beside one and took a photograph. That single picture is the reason most of the world has heard of the yeti at all. People have been arguing about what made that print ever since — which tells you something important: one photo is not the same thing as an answer.

🧬 Somebody Finally Tested the Hair Tap to reveal ▾

For years, museums and collectors kept bones, teeth, and clumps of hair labelled "yeti." In 2017 a research team led by biologist Charlotte Lindqvist ran DNA tests on nine of those samples. Almost every one came back as a bear — Himalayan brown bear, Tibetan brown bear, Asian black bear. One turned out to be a dog. The test didn't just say "not a yeti." It said which real animal was standing there instead.

☀️ Footprints Grow During the Day Tap to reveal ▾

Here is a thing almost nobody knows. When sunlight hits a footprint in snow, the edges melt outward. The hole gets wider, shallower, and blurrier by the hour. A normal fox print left at dawn can look enormous and monstrous by mid-afternoon. That's why researchers photograph tracks with a ruler beside them and write down the time — because a track measured at 3 p.m. is telling you a slightly different story than the same track at 6 a.m.

🐆 The Ghost of the Mountains Is Real Tap to reveal ▾

People who live in the high Himalaya call the snow leopard the ghost of the mountains, and they are not exaggerating — its grey, spotted coat vanishes against rock so completely that researchers can look straight at one and miss it. Scientists think only a few thousand are left, spread across about twelve countries. Many researchers spend years setting camera traps before they get a single clear photo of one.

The Tracks Behind Our House

Part 1 — Bigger Than Any Boot

Pema Dolkar found them on the first clear morning after the storm.

Tracks. A whole line of them, running along the stone wall behind her family's house and up toward the ridge, pressed into snow so new that nothing else had touched it. Pema set her own boot down beside one and stared. Two of her boots, heel to toe, would have fit inside a single print. At the front there were rounded lumps that could almost — almost — have been toes.

Ranglung was a small village, high enough that clouds sometimes sat below it like a second valley. Everyone had heard about the tracks before lunch.

Her older brother Tashi laughed so hard he had to sit down on the woodpile.

Her grandmother, Ama Chodon, did not laugh. "People have watched that ridge for a very long time," she said. "The stories about it are older than this house. They are older than me, and that is saying something."

That night Pema lay awake and heard it: a low coughing grunt, close by, somewhere past the woodpile. Then a shadow crossed her window — a shape wide enough to darken the whole square of glass — and was gone.

By morning Pema was certain. There was a yeti on the ridge above her house.

Part 2 — "Show Me Something I Can Measure"

Dr. Sonam Yangzom arrived four days later with two metal cases and a bad cold. She had grown up two valleys over. For six years she had been trying to photograph snow leopards, which she described as the most elusive animal she had ever worked with.

Pema told her everything in one breath: the tracks, the grunt, the shadow at the window.

Sonam did not laugh either. She also did not agree. "I'm skeptical," she said, blowing her nose. "That is not the same as saying you're wrong. It means I don't believe it yet. Show me something I can measure."

Ama Chodon poured the tea. "Keep both," she told Pema. "The old stories tell you what this mountain means to the people who live under it, and they have done that job well for hundreds of years. The measuring tells you what walked past your window on Tuesday night. Don't ask one to do the other's work."

Part 3 — The Plan

They made a list, and Sonam wrote it in pencil so that it could change.

1. Photograph every print with a ruler lying beside it.
2. Measure the same print twice — once at sunrise, once in the afternoon.
3. Follow the whole trail. One print is a guess; a hundred prints are evidence.
4. Set two camera traps: one at the wall, one at the top of the ridge.

Everything Sonam did was slow and deliberate. She brushed loose snow off the prints with a paintbrush and wrote the time beside every photograph. When Pema asked why it took so long, Sonam said, "Because I have to verify all of this later, and future-me gets extremely annoyed when present-me guesses."

Part 4 — What the Snow Said

At sunrise the biggest print measured seventeen centimetres, with sharp clean edges.

At three in the afternoon the exact same print measured twenty-six centimetres, with edges that were soft and rounded and blurry.

Pema measured it three times before she would believe it. "It grew."

"The sun melted the sides," Sonam said. "A track in snow gets bigger all day, and mushier, until it looks like nothing that ever lived. A lot of the giant footprints people have photographed were ordinary prints that spent an afternoon in the sunshine."

Then Sonam found where the trail crossed a patch of snow that stayed in shade all day, and knelt. In the shade, the print was not one shape at all. It was two distinct shapes overlapping — a smaller oval, and a longer one landing right on top of it.

"Back foot coming down inside the front foot's print," she said. "Bears do that when they walk. Two prints stack up and make one monster."

The camera at the wall answered the rest of it. On the little screen, at 2:14 in the morning, a Himalayan brown bear ambled past the woodpile — thick, humped at the shoulder, entirely unbothered — and coughed once.

The ridge camera took eleven nights. Then, on a frame that Sonam stared at without speaking for a very long time, a snow leopard walked out of the dark, looked straight into the lens, and walked on.

Part 5 — Rarer Than a Monster

Pema went quiet for most of a day. She had wanted a yeti. What she had was a bear, a cat, and a lot of arithmetic about melting snow.

"You're disappointed," Sonam said. "Good. That is what a real conclusion feels like sometimes. It doesn't always feel like winning."

"It's just a bear," Pema said.

"There may be only a few thousand snow leopards left in the entire world, spread across about twelve countries. In some places researchers count the brown bears in the low hundreds. I have been setting cameras for six years for the photograph that you and I took eleven nights ago." Sonam turned the screen around. "You didn't find a monster, Pema. You found two of the rarest animals on the planet standing in your grandmother's yard."

Ama Chodon looked up from the fire. "The mountain didn't get any smaller," she said. "You got closer to it."

Pema pinned the photograph above her bed and wrote the date under it. Then she started a notebook, and on the first page she wrote the only rule that mattered: measure it twice — and once in the shade.

1. What three things made Pema certain there was a yeti? ▶
Huge tracks along the stone wall (two of her boots would fit inside one print), a low coughing grunt near the woodpile at night, and a shadow wide enough to darken her whole window as something moved past it.
2. Sonam says, "I'm skeptical. That is not the same as saying you're wrong." What does she mean? ▶
She is not calling Pema a liar and she is not making fun of her. She is saying she has not seen proof yet, so she is holding off on deciding. Being skeptical means keeping the question open until evidence arrives — which is why she immediately asks for something she can measure instead of just walking away.
3. Why did the same footprint measure seventeen centimetres in the morning and twenty-six in the afternoon? ▶
Sunlight melted the edges of the print, so the hole in the snow spread wider, shallower, and blurrier as the day went on. Nothing about the animal changed — only the snow did. This is exactly why Sonam insisted on measuring twice and writing down the time.
4. What did the shaded patch of snow reveal, and why did it matter so much? ▶
Because that snow never got direct sun, the print had not melted — and there it showed up as two distinct overlapping shapes instead of one giant one. A bear's back foot lands inside the print its front foot just made, so two ordinary prints stack up and look like one monstrous one. The shade preserved the evidence the sunshine had been erasing everywhere else.
5. What is the THEME (big idea) of the story? ▶
Careful evidence doesn't shrink the world — it makes it bigger. Pema trades a monster she imagined for two animals so rare that a scientist waited six years to photograph one of them. Ama Chodon says it best: "The mountain didn't get any smaller. You got closer to it." The story also respects the old stories: Ama Chodon says to keep BOTH, because stories and measurements do different jobs.

Author's Purpose
10 min

🥧 What Is "Author's Purpose"?

Every single thing you read was written by a person who wanted something to happen in your head. That "something" is the author's purpose — the reason they bothered to write it at all.

There are three main ones, and they spell a word you will never forget:

🥧 Persuade · Inform · Entertain

📣 PERSUADE
Wants you to agree or do something. Ads, letters to the editor, "vote for me," "you should try this."
📊 INFORM
Wants to teach you facts. Textbooks, news articles, recipes, the back of a cereal box, a museum sign.
🎭 ENTERTAIN
Wants you to enjoy it. Stories, poems, comics, jokes, novels, anything with characters in it.

Every purpose leaves clues. Learn the clues and you never have to guess.

📣 PERSUADE clues: opinion words (best, worst, terrible, amazing) · you should · we must · don't miss · it asks you to do something

📊 INFORM clues: facts you could check · numbers, dates, measurements · headings and sections · words like because, causes, is made of · no characters, no opinions

🎭 ENTERTAIN clues: characters with names · dialogue in quotation marks · description you can picture · a problem and a surprise · it makes you laugh or worry

One short example of each:

📣 "Everyone in this school should learn to read a snow gauge. It takes ten minutes, and it is the most useful ten minutes of your life. Sign up today."
→ PERSUADE. "should," "most useful," and a direct order: sign up.

📊 "A snow gauge is a tube marked along the side. Snow falls in, and a reader checks the mark it reaches. The village gauge is read at seven o'clock each morning."
→ INFORM. Facts and a time. Nobody's opinion appears anywhere.

🎭 "'Snow tonight,' Tashi announced, tapping his kneecap. Outside, the goats were sunbathing on a wall under a cloudless sky. It snowed for eleven hours."
→ ENTERTAIN. A character, dialogue, and a joke at the end.

The trap that catches everybody:

🪤 A text can have TWO purposes. A magazine article about bears can open with a joke (entertain) and then spend four paragraphs on facts (inform). When that happens, look for the MAIN purpose — the job the text spends the most space doing — and call the other one the secondary purpose.
🪤 Facts can be used to persuade. Numbers do not automatically mean "inform." If the facts are there as reasons why you should do something, the purpose is still to persuade.

Golden rule: don't just name the purpose — point at the exact words that gave it away.

✏️ Your Writing Prompt

Part 1 — Same mountain, two purposes. Write two short paragraphs about the same snowy mountain. Label them.
📊 INFORM: facts only — how deep the snow gets, what animals live there, how cold it is, a number or two. No characters, no opinions.
🎭 ENTERTAIN: put a person on that mountain. Give them a name, let them say something out loud, and let something happen.

Part 2 — Reflection. Write about a time evidence changed your mind — you were sure about something, then you looked closely (or somebody showed you proof) and you had to change your answer. What did you believe? What was the evidence? How did it feel to be wrong? Use at least 3 vocabulary words. (At least 5 sentences total.)
