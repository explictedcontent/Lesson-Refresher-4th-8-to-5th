The Giants of Africa and Asia

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

Elephants are the largest animals that walk on land, and there are two main kinds. The African elephant is the bigger of the two. Both the males and the females grow long tusks, and its huge ears are shaped a bit like the continent of Africa. The Asian elephant is smaller, with rounder ears, and usually only some of the males grow tusks at all.

These two kinds live far apart. African elephants roam across the wide grassy savannas and thick forests of Africa, south of the Sahara Desert. Asian elephants live in the forests and grasslands of India and Southeast Asia. Even though they are different species, both live in the wild only on those two continents — not in North America, Europe, or Australia.

Species: a particular kind of animal.   Savanna: a wide, grassy plain with a few trees.   Matriarch: the oldest female who leads a herd.   Migration: traveling from one place to another.   Conservation: protecting nature so it survives.

Elephants live in close family groups called herds. Each herd is led by the oldest and wisest female, called the matriarch. She remembers where to find water and food, even during hard, dry times, and the whole family trusts her memory. Young male elephants often leave the herd as they grow up, while the females usually stay together for life.

People and elephants have shared a long history. In Asia, elephants have worked alongside people for over 4,000 years — carrying heavy loads, helping move logs in the forests, and marching in grand parades and religious festivals. In India, the beloved elephant-headed god Ganesha is honored by millions of people. Long ago, armies even used elephants in battle — the famous general Hannibal once marched war elephants across the snowy Alps mountains.

Because of all this, elephants stand for wisdom, memory, and strength in many cultures. But these gentle giants face real dangers. The biggest threat is ivory poaching — hunters killing elephants just for their tusks, which are made of ivory. Habitat loss, as forests and grasslands are cleared for farms and cities, is another serious problem.

The good news is that people are fighting back to protect them. Many countries have banned the ivory trade, so tusks can no longer be sold. Special reserves and national parks now guard the land where elephants live. Rangers work to stop poachers, and conservationists try to keep migration paths open so herds can still travel safely between water and food. This kind of conservation gives elephants a real chance to survive for the future.
