The Ocean's Ultimate Superhero

Head-Foot: What Is a Cephalopod?

The octopus belongs to a group of animals called cephalopods, a word that means "head-foot." That name is perfect: on an octopus, the head connects directly to the arms, with no neck or body in between. Its eight arms grow right out of its head. Squid and cuttlefish are cephalopods too, but the octopus may be the strangest and smartest of them all.

Three Hearts and Blue Blood

An octopus has three hearts. Two of them pump blood to the gills, where the blood picks up oxygen. The third heart pumps that oxygen-rich blood to the rest of the body. Stranger still, octopus blood is blue, not red. Our blood is red because it uses iron to carry oxygen. Octopus blood uses a copper-based molecule called hemocyanin instead, and copper turns the blood blue. Hemocyanin works especially well in the cold, low-oxygen water of the deep sea.

Nine Brains

Here is one of the most amazing facts of all: an octopus has nine brains. There is one central brain, plus a smaller cluster of nerves called a ganglion in each of the eight arms. This means each arm can think and act partly on its own. An arm can keep exploring a crack in the rocks even while the central brain is busy paying attention to something completely different.

The Master of Camouflage

The octopus is one of the greatest quick-change artists in nature. Its skin is packed with color-changing cells called chromatophores that let it change color in under one second. It can also raise tiny bumps in its skin called papillae to change its texture, making its skin smooth like sand or spiky like coral. So it can match both the color AND the texture of rocks and coral around it. The wildest part? Octopuses are colorblind — they cannot see color at all. Scientists still do not fully understand how a colorblind animal matches colors so perfectly.

Ink Cloud and Jet Propulsion

When a predator gets too close, an octopus can shoot out a cloud of dark ink. This ink cloud works like a smoke screen, hiding the octopus so it can escape. The ink even confuses the predator's sense of smell, so the hunter cannot track it. To rocket away fast, the octopus uses jet propulsion: it pulls water into its body and then blasts it out through a tube called the siphon, shooting itself backward like a living jet engine.

No Bones, No Problem

An octopus has no bones anywhere in its body. The only hard part is its beak. This means an octopus can squeeze its whole soft body through any gap larger than its beak — even a hole the size of a quarter! If an octopus loses an arm to a predator, that is not the end of it either: the arm slowly grows back over several months, a power called regeneration.

One of the Smartest Animals in the Sea

Octopuses are remarkably intelligent. In laboratories they have learned to open jars to reach food inside, and they can solve mazes. Some wild octopuses collect empty coconut shells and carry them around to hide inside later — that is real tool use, which very few animals can do. They can even recognize individual humans, treating some keepers as friends and squirting water at ones they dislike. Yet for all their brilliance, octopuses have short lives — most species live only 1 to 5 years.
