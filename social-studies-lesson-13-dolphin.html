Dolphins Around the World

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

Dolphins are some of the smartest and most playful animals in the sea, and they live almost everywhere. They are marine mammals, found in every ocean on Earth — from warm tropical seas to chilly coastal waters near the poles. A few kinds don't even live in the ocean at all: river dolphins swim in the muddy waters of the Amazon River in South America and the Ganges River in Asia.

There are around 40 species of dolphin. They range from the tiny Maui dolphin, one of the smallest, all the way up to the huge orca, or killer whale. Many people are surprised to learn that the orca is actually the largest member of the dolphin family, even though its name says "whale."

Mammal: an animal that breathes air and feeds its babies milk.   Pod: a group that dolphins live and travel in.   Cooperation: working together to reach a goal.   Migration: moving from one place to another with the seasons.   Conservation: protecting nature so it survives.

Dolphins are very social. They live in groups called pods, and sometimes many pods gather into a "superpod" of hundreds of dolphins. Inside a pod they cooperate to hunt fish and to protect one another from danger. Working as a team is a big part of how dolphins survive.

People have admired dolphins for thousands of years. The ancient Greeks and Romans told stories and made art about them, and sailors believed a dolphin swimming beside a ship was good luck. Coins from ancient Greece were even stamped with pictures of dolphins.

In a few special places, people and dolphins actually work together. In Laguna, Brazil, wild dolphins herd fish toward fishermen standing in the shallow water, then give a signal — a sudden dive or a slap of the tail — that tells the people exactly when to cast their nets. This amazing partnership has been passed down for generations, and similar teamwork was once recorded in Myanmar in Asia.

Dolphins have helped people in other ways too. Some navies have trained dolphins to find objects hidden underwater, and there are reports of wild dolphins helping swimmers who were in trouble. Because people value dolphins so much, many countries now work to protect them. The tuna industry even created "dolphin-safe" fishing rules after too many dolphins were being caught in fishing nets.

Dolphins still face real dangers. Pollution, fishing nets (which trap them by accident, called bycatch), and busy boat traffic all threaten them. But conservation groups and marine parks around the world keep working to keep these clever, friendly animals safe.
