Masters of Madagascar and Beyond

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

Chameleons are famous for changing color, but here is something just as amazing: where they live on Earth. Almost all chameleons live in the Eastern Hemisphere, mostly in Africa — and especially on the giant island of Madagascar. Madagascar sits off Africa's southeast coast, and it is home to about half of the world's 200+ chameleon species. That is an incredible number of chameleons for one island!

Chameleons also live in a few other places in the Eastern Hemisphere: southern Europe (like parts of Spain), the Middle East, and parts of southern Asia. But there is one big place you will not find a wild chameleon: the Americas. No chameleon species lives naturally in North or South America.

Island: land with water all around it.   Biodiversity: the huge variety of living things in a place.   Endemic: a species found naturally in only one place.   Habitat: the home where a plant or animal lives.   Conservation: protecting nature so it survives.

Why does one island have so many chameleons? The answer is Madagascar's amazing biodiversity. Millions of years ago, Madagascar broke away from the other land around it. Cut off by ocean, its plants and animals kept changing on their own until many became endemic — found nowhere else on Earth. Famous Madagascar animals like lemurs, and most of its chameleons, live only there. It is like a giant natural laboratory floating in the sea.

Most chameleons make their habitat in trees and bushes inside rainforests and savannas, where they climb slowly and grip branches with their curled tails. A few tough species even live in deserts. Some chameleons are huge, while others — the tiny Brookesia species — are so small they can sit on the tip of your finger.

People and chameleons have shared Madagascar for a long time. In many Malagasy (Madagascar) traditions, chameleons are respected, and there are local beliefs and taboos, called "fady," about them. In other cultures around the world, the chameleon has become a symbol of change and patience, because it moves carefully and shifts its colors.

Today chameleons need help. Madagascar's rainforests are being cut down for farming and logging, and when the forest disappears, so does chameleon habitat. Some chameleons are also over-collected for the worldwide pet trade. The good news is that conservation is working: national parks protect large areas of habitat, and new rules now limit how many chameleons can be taken from the wild. Because chameleons from Africa are kept as pets all over the globe, protecting the wild ones matters more than ever.
