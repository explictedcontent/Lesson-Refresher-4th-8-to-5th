The Yeti: The Roof of the World

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

People call the Himalayas the "roof of the world," and for once a nickname is not an exaggeration. This is the highest mountain range on Earth, a wall of rock and ice about 2,400 kilometres long that crosses five countries: Nepal, India, Bhutan, China (including the region of Tibet), and Pakistan. Somewhere in the stories told in these mountains walks the yeti — a tall, shaggy figure in the snow. We will get to the yeti. But the yeti only makes sense once you know the place it comes from, and the place is far more astonishing than the legend.

Start with the mountain. Mount Everest is the highest point on the planet, about 8,849 metres above sea level — roughly 29,032 feet. It sits right on the border between Nepal and Tibet, so it belongs to two sides at once, and it has a name on each side. In Nepali it is Sagarmatha. In Tibetan it is Chomolungma. The English name honours George Everest, a British surveyor, and it was given by outsiders in 1865 — long after local people already had names of their own. That is worth pausing on: when you learn a place name, it is fair to ask who named it, and what did the people who live there call it first?

Why is it so high? This is real earth science, and it is not complicated. The outer shell of the Earth is broken into huge slabs called tectonic plates, and they drift — slowly, only a few centimetres a year, about the speed your fingernails grow. Around 50 million years ago the plate carrying India finished crossing an ancient ocean and slammed into the plate carrying Asia. Neither one could sink under the other, so the rock between them had nowhere to go but up. Squeeze a rug from both ends and it wrinkles; that is exactly what happened to the crust, only in stone and over millions of years. And here is the wild part: the collision never stopped. India is still pushing north, so the Himalayas are still rising a few millimetres every year. The same squeeze is why earthquakes shake Nepal and northern India — a serious, ongoing fact of life there, not ancient history. You can even find marine fossils, the shells of sea creatures, in rock high on Everest, because that stone was once the floor of an ocean.

Tectonic plate: a giant slab of Earth's outer shell that slowly moves.   Summit: the very top of a mountain.   Altitude: height above sea level.   Terrace: a flat step cut into a steep slope for farming.   Ethnic group: a people who share ancestry, language, and traditions.   Folklore: the traditional stories and beliefs a community passes down.   Permit: official permission a government gives before you may do something.

The Sherpa people. Now meet the people, and get this part right, because most of the world gets it wrong. The Sherpa are an ethnic group — a people — from the mountainous regions of eastern Nepal, especially the high valleys of the Khumbu near Everest. Their ancestors moved south from Tibet several hundred years ago, crossing the high passes. They speak their own language, also called Sherpa, and most follow Tibetan Buddhism, with monasteries, prayer flags, and festivals that shape the year. Many Sherpas are famous mountaineers, guides, and high-altitude porters, and they are extremely good at it. But "Sherpa" is not a job title. It is like "Scottish" or "Navajo" — the name of a people, not a description of work. Calling every mountain porter a "sherpa" erases a whole culture and turns a name into equipment. Sherpas are farmers, traders, monks, teachers, cooks, students, and business owners too.

1953: a shared achievement. On 29 May 1953, Tenzing Norgay, a Sherpa climber, and Edmund Hillary, a beekeeper from New Zealand, became the first climbers confirmed to reach the summit of Everest. They got there together, roped to one another, each depending completely on the other. Afterwards reporters kept demanding to know which man stepped on the top first, as if it were a race between two people. Both men refused to split it. Tenzing Norgay called it a team effort; Hillary said they reached it as a team. When you tell this story, tell it the way they told it: two people, one climb, one achievement.

Living high up. The Himalayas are not empty scenery — millions of people live in and around them, and geography shapes almost everything about daily life. The land is steep, so farmers build terraces: flat steps cut into the hillside like a giant staircase, which hold the soil in place and stop rain from washing it downhill. On those steps they grow barley, buckwheat, potatoes, and rice lower down. Higher up, where crops will barely grow, families keep yaks — big, shaggy, wonderfully suited to thin cold air. Yaks give milk (which becomes butter and cheese), wool for rope and clothing, dung for fuel where there are no trees, and above all muscle: a yak can carry a heavy load along a narrow trail where no truck could ever drive.

Because the mountains are a wall, the few gaps through them matter enormously. For centuries, traders led caravans over high passes between Nepal and Tibet, carrying salt from Tibetan lakes down to the valleys and hauling grain back up. Those trade routes moved goods, but they also moved languages, religion, recipes, and news — which is why cultures on both sides of the range share so much. Weather rules the calendar: monsoon rains in summer, deep snow that closes passes in winter, and a short climbing and trading season in between. Altitude rules the body too. The air on Everest holds about a third as much oxygen as air at sea level, so visitors get headaches, breathe fast, and must climb slowly to let their bodies adjust. Many people whose families have lived at high altitude for thousands of years — including Sherpas and Tibetans — carry inherited adaptations that help them use oxygen efficiently up there. Scientists have found genetic differences that keep their blood from thickening the way a visitor's does. It is not magic and it is not just practice; it is real biology shaped by living in a real place.

And now the yeti. In the folklore of the Himalayas there are stories of a large, hairy being in the snow and rock above the villages. Different communities tell different versions, and in some Sherpa and Tibetan traditions the creature is a serious figure — something powerful, connected to the wild places, not a cartoon. When British visitors in the 1920s heard a Tibetan phrase for such a being, a newspaper translated it loosely as "Abominable Snowman," and that catchy nickname flew around the world. Explorers came hunting for footprints; magazines printed photographs of tracks in the snow. Scientists who have tested "yeti" hair and bone samples have generally traced them back to known animals such as bears. So: there is no confirmed animal behind the legend. But notice how you say that. A traditional story is not a lie, and laughing at someone's folklore is a poor way to learn about them. The yeti is part of a living culture, and it deserves the same respect you would want for the stories your own family tells. The honest sentence is: this is a traditional story that matters to people, and there is no scientific evidence of the animal itself. Both halves are true.

Sharing a mountain. Fame has a cost. After 1953, mountaineering and tourism brought the world to Everest. Today, trekking and climbing are a huge part of Nepal's economy, providing jobs and income for guides, cooks, porters, lodge owners, and pilots. But on good-weather days hundreds of climbers can be waiting to use the same narrow ridge, and crowding at that altitude is dangerous — every extra hour in the "death zone" above about 8,000 metres burns through oxygen and strength. Decades of expeditions also left litter: empty oxygen bottles, torn tents, food packaging, and human waste, in a place too cold and dry for rubbish to break down.

This is where civics walks onto the mountain. Everest is a shared place, and shared places need rules that people agree to follow. Nepal requires an expensive permit to climb, which limits who goes and raises money for the region. Climbing teams must leave a rubbish deposit — money they only get back if they carry their waste down with them. Nepal created Sagarmatha National Park around the mountain in 1976 to protect it. Local organisations run clean-up expeditions that haul tonnes of old gear off the slopes, and Sherpa-led teams do most of that dangerous work. There is real argument about what else should change — fewer permits? stricter experience requirements? — and that argument is exactly what governing a shared place looks like. Which brings you back to where this started. The Himalayas were built by forces nobody controls. What happens on them now is decided by people, together.
