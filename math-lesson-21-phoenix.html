📋 Two Words That Get Mixed Up Constantly

Take the number 12.

Numbers that go INTO 12 evenly are its factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Notice they are all 12 or smaller.

Numbers you land on when you skip-count BY 12 are its multiples: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60… Those are all 12 or bigger, and the list never ends.

Same number, two totally different lists. Factors go down. Multiples go up. Keep that in your head for the whole lesson.

🔎 Phase 2 — The Factor Finder (12 min)

Learn the pairing method, then hunt down every factor of 6 different numbers.

📋 What a Factor Is, and How to Find Them All

A factor of a number divides into it evenly — nothing left over, no remainder. Factors always come in pairs, because every division has a partner multiplication.

12 = 1 × 12  =  2 × 6  =  3 × 4

So the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 — six of them.

1
Start at 1. It is a factor of every number, and its partner is the number itself.
2
Try 2, then 3, then 4… in order. Each time it divides evenly, write down both numbers in the pair.
3
Stop when the pairs meet. Once the number you are testing gets as big as its own partner, you already have every factor.
Worked example — every factor of 18
1 × 181 always works; partner is 18 itself
2 × 918 is even, so 2 divides in
3 × 618 ÷ 3 = 6, no remainder
4?  18 ÷ 4 = 4 remainder 2 ✗not a factor — skip it
5?  18 ÷ 5 = 3 remainder 3 ✗not a factor — skip it
6?  its partner is 3 — but we already wrote 3 × 6the pairs have MET, so we stop here
Factors of 18 = 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18  (6 factors)
Careful with square numbers. For 16 the pairs are 1 × 16, 2 × 8, and 4 × 4. That last pair is the same number twice, so 4 only gets counted once. Factors of 16 = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — that is five factors, an odd amount. Square numbers are the only numbers with an odd number of factors.
Prime or composite? Count the factors when you are finished.
• Exactly two factors (1 and itself) → the number is PRIME. Example: 13 = 1 × 13 only.
• More than two factors → the number is COMPOSITE. Example: 12 has six.
• The number 1 has only one factor, so it is NEITHER prime nor composite. It is in a club of its own.

🧮 Phase 3 — Prime Hunter (12 min)

Multiples are the other side of the coin. Use them to knock out every composite number up to 30.

📋 Multiples — and Why They Are NOT Factors

A multiple of a number is what you get when you multiply it by 1, 2, 3, 4… In other words, it is where you land when you skip-count.

Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42 …

FACTORS of 6

  • 1, 2, 3, 6
  • Divide IN to 6
  • Never bigger than 6
  • A short list that ENDS
  • Found by dividing

MULTIPLES of 6

  • 6, 12, 18, 24, …
  • Count UP from 6
  • Never smaller than 6
  • A list that NEVER ends
  • Found by multiplying
One fact, two ways of saying it. "3 is a factor of 12" and "12 is a multiple of 3" mean exactly the same thing. The small number is the factor; the big number is the multiple. If someone tells you 12 is a factor of 3, they have it backwards — 12 cannot fit inside 3.
Divisibility shortcuts — quick ways to spot a factor without dividing.
• 2 → the number ends in 0, 2, 4, 6 or 8 (it is even). 84 ends in 4 ✓
• 5 → the number ends in 0 or 5. 435 ends in 5 ✓ (435 ÷ 5 = 87)
• 10 → the number ends in 0. 430 ✓, but 435 ✗
• 3 → add up the digits; if that total is a multiple of 3, so is the number. 84 → 8 + 4 = 12, and 12 is a multiple of 3 ✓ (84 ÷ 3 = 28)
Notice that anything divisible by 10 must also be divisible by both 2 and 5.
Why 2 is the only even prime. Every even number has 2 as a factor. For the number 2 itself that is fine — its factors are just 1 and 2, exactly two of them. But 4, 6, 8, 10 and every other even number get 2 as an extra factor on top of 1 and themselves, which pushes them past two factors. So they are all composite. Every other prime has to be odd.
⚠️ Odd does NOT mean prime. Loads of odd numbers are composite: 9 = 3 × 3, 15 = 3 × 5, 21 = 3 × 7, 25 = 5 × 5, 27 = 3 × 9. Always count the factors — do not guess from odd or even.

🤝 Phase 4 — Sharing: GCF & LCM (12 min)

What happens when TWO numbers share factors — or share multiples. Then 6 practice questions.

📋 Greatest Common Factor (GCF)

List the factors of both numbers, look for the ones that appear in both lists, and take the biggest one.

Worked example — GCF of 12 and 18
12:  1 2 3 4 6 12
18:  1 2 3 6 9 18
Shared: 1, 2, 3, 6these four appear in both lists
GCF = 6

📋 Least Common Multiple (LCM)

Skip-count each number and keep going until you hit the first number that shows up on both lists.

Worked example — LCM of 4 and 6
4:  4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24 …
6:  6, 12, 18, 24 …
First one they share: 1224 is shared too, but 12 comes first
LCM = 12
A sanity check that catches most mistakes. The GCF is about factors, so it is small — it can never be bigger than the smaller of your two numbers. The LCM is about multiples, so it is big — it can never be smaller than the larger of your two numbers. For 12 and 18: GCF 6 (≤ 12 ✓) and LCM 36 (≥ 18 ✓). If your GCF comes out huge, you found a multiple by mistake.
