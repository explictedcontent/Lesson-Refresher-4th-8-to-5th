The Kraken: Sailors, Maps, and Sea Monsters

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

Picture a wooden ship, no engine, no radio, no satellite map. The crew is somewhere in the cold gray water between Norway and Iceland, out of sight of land, and something enormous moves under the hull. What do you write down when you get home? For hundreds of years, the answer in the far north was one word: the kraken. This lesson is really about a bigger question, though — how do we know what we know? Some of what those sailors reported turned out to be true. Some of it turned out to be a story. Learning to tell the difference is one of the most useful skills a person can have.

Start with the place. The kraken legend belongs to the sailors of Norway and Iceland. Norway, Sweden, and Denmark together make up the region called Scandinavia, in northern Europe; Iceland is a separate island nation far out in the North Atlantic Ocean. Look at Norway on a map and you will see a coastline that looks like it was cut with a saw. Those long, deep, narrow inlets are fjords, carved by glaciers during the ice ages and later flooded by the sea. All that folding and cutting makes Norway's coastline astonishingly long — many thousands of kilometers for a fairly small country. Between Norway and Iceland lies the Norwegian Sea, and beyond it the wider North Atlantic.

Geography shapes how people live. Norway is mountainous, and for most of its history the easiest road was water. Families settled on the flat land at the head of a fjord, fished the cold sea, and travelled by boat because travelling by land meant climbing mountains. When your food, your news, your trade, and your neighbors all arrive by sea, you become excellent at ships — and you tell a lot of stories about what is out there.

Fjord: a long, deep, narrow arm of the sea carved by a glacier.   Navigate: to figure out where you are and steer where you want to go.   Archaeology: the study of the past through objects and places people left behind.   Evidence: proof you can check.   Legend: a traditional story that may or may not be true.   Source: anything — a book, a map, an object — that gives us information about the past.

The Norse seafarers. The people of Scandinavia during the years from roughly 793 to 1066 CE are usually called the Norse, and that stretch of time is called the Viking Age. Here is where a lot of movies get it wrong. Most Norse people were farmers — they grew barley and oats, kept cattle and sheep, and worried about the weather like farmers everywhere. Many were traders who carried furs, walrus ivory, iron, and amber along rivers all the way to Constantinople and the Middle East; archaeologists still dig up Middle Eastern silver coins in Scandinavian soil. They were also craftspeople, poets, and lawmakers: in Iceland the Alþingi, an assembly where free men gathered to make and argue law, was founded around 930 CE, and it is one of the oldest lasting assemblies in the world. And yes — some of them were raiders, who attacked coastal towns and monasteries, took treasure, and captured people to sell as slaves. All of that is true at once. Real people are not one thing, and a good historian refuses to shrink them down to one.

The ships. The Norse were superb shipbuilders. The famous longship was built of overlapping planks, which made it light, flexible, and strong in heavy waves. It carried a big square sail and oars, so it worked with or without wind. Best of all it had a shallow bottom: it barely sat down into the water, so the same ship that had just crossed open ocean could turn up a shallow river or be dragged straight onto a beach without a harbor. For hauling cargo and families to new lands, the Norse used a different ship, the knarr — wider, deeper, slower, built to carry weight rather than warriors.

Sailing west. Step by step, Norse crews pushed across the North Atlantic. They reached the Faroe Islands in the 800s, began settling Iceland around 874 CE, and around 985 CE a man known as Erik the Red led settlers to Greenland. Then, around the year 1000 CE, Leif Erikson sailed further west still and landed in North America — roughly five hundred years before Columbus crossed the Atlantic. For a long time that was only a saga, a story written down centuries later. Then in 1960, archaeologists Helge Ingstad and Anne Stine Ingstad found the remains of a Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, on the northern tip of Newfoundland in Canada: turf building foundations, iron nails, a bronze cloak pin, and a workshop where bog iron had been worked. That is archaeological evidence — physical proof you can dig up, date, and let other scientists check. The story became history because someone found the receipts.

Navigating without instruments. Here is the part that should genuinely impress you: the Norse crossed that ocean before the magnetic compass reached Europe. The compass was being used for navigation in China earlier, and European sailors only began writing about it around the 1100s and 1200s CE — after the Viking Age was over. So how did they do it? They used the sun, checking its height at midday to judge how far north they were. They used the stars at night. They memorized coastal landmarks — this mountain, that headland, that island shaped like a tooth. They read ocean currents and the direction of the swell. They watched birds and whales, because seabirds that fly out and come back mean land is near; one famous sailor, Hrafna-Flóki, is said to have released ravens and followed the one that did not return. This was not luck. It was knowledge, learned, tested, and passed down.

Now the monsters. Around 1250 CE, a Norwegian book called the King's Mirror — written as a father teaching his son about the world — described a creature called the hafgufa, so vast that sailors mistook it for an island. Five centuries later, in 1752, a Norwegian bishop named Erik Pontoppidan wrote a natural history of Norway that described the kraken in the same way: a floating-island-sized beast whose back broke the surface, with arms that could reach a ship's mast. Pontoppidan was not trying to write a scary story. He believed he was collecting facts, gathered from the fishermen he trusted. That is exactly what makes old sources tricky: an honest person can carefully write down something that is not correct.

And the maps. In 1539, a Swedish writer named Olaus Magnus published the Carta Marina, a huge, gorgeous map of Scandinavia and the northern seas — and he filled that water with sea monsters: coiling serpents, spouting beasts, a creature swallowing a ship. Why would a serious mapmaker do that? Historians who study old maps give three honest reasons. One: to warn. A monster drawn over a stretch of sea often marked water that was dangerous or barely explored. Two: to fill blank space. Empty ocean looked unfinished, and a map with monsters was a map people wanted to buy. Three: to record real animals seen badly. A whale surfacing in fog, a walrus with its tusks, a squid's arm — glimpsed for two seconds from a pitching deck, described from memory years later, then drawn by an artist who had never left dry land. Copy that drawing a few times and you have a monster. (By the way, the famous phrase "here be dragons" almost never appears on real maps. It shows up on one small globe from about 1510, and that is it.)

So a map is a source — and sources mix things together. The Carta Marina holds real coastlines, real towns, real information about the north, and also serpents that never existed, all on one sheet of paper. Learning to read a source means asking: who made this, when, how did they know, and which parts can I check?

The twist at the end. Deep in the ocean lives an animal called the giant squid. It is real. It can reach about 13 meters long counting its two long feeding tentacles, and it has the largest eyes of any animal on Earth, roughly the width of a dinner plate — useful for spotting the faint shapes of sperm whales, which hunt them. Sperm whales surface with round sucker-shaped scars on their skin, evidence of fights nobody was there to watch. For centuries, almost every giant squid humans saw was dead or dying at the surface, which is a terrible way to meet an animal. Scientists did not photograph a living giant squid in the wild until 2004, when Japanese researchers finally got pictures in the deep water off the Ogasawara Islands, and it was filmed in its deep-sea home in 2012.

So was the kraken "real"? Not as a ship-swallowing island. But those sailors were not making everything up either. They were reporting something enormous and unfamiliar with the only words they had — and it took modern science more than 250 years after Pontoppidan to get a clear photograph of the animal underneath the legend. That is the big idea of this lesson. Exploration turned blank space on maps into charted coastlines, and evidence turned some legends into history and left others as stories. Both parts matter. Being curious is what sends people out; checking the evidence is what turns what they bring back into knowledge.
