Reading Passage
10 min

Joey's First Leap

Joey was a young kangaroo who had a problem. He had grown far too big for his mother Willa's warm pouch, but the thought of hopping on his own made his stomach flip. Out on the wide red outback, the other young kangaroos in the mob bounded across the sand as if they were flying. Beside them, Joey felt clumsy and timid.

"Come on, little one," Willa said gently one morning. She nudged him out onto the open ground. "You can't stay in the pouch forever. Trust your own legs."

Joey swallowed hard and pushed off. His first hop was a disaster — he tipped sideways, tumbled, and landed in a cloud of dust. A few of the other joeys giggled, and Joey felt his ears go hot with embarrassed heat. He wanted to crawl right back into the pouch and hide.

An old gray kangaroo named Barnaby hopped over slowly. He had watched the whole thing. "I'll tell you the secret," Barnaby said kindly. "Don't look down at your feet. Look ahead, out at the horizon, and let your springy legs do the work. And you must be persistent — keep trying, even when it feels impossible."

So Joey practiced. All through the long, hot day he hopped and fell, hopped and fell. But little by little, something began to change. His wobbly bounces grew steady, and then they grew strong. He stopped watching his feet and lifted his eyes to the horizon, just as Barnaby had said. His tail found its rhythm, thumping like a drum.

Late that afternoon, the sky turned a strange brown color. A sudden dust storm came rolling across the plain, and the whole mob turned to flee. Joey's heart pounded. This was the real test. He pushed off — and this time his legs carried him swiftly, bound after powerful bound, right alongside the grown kangaroos. For the very first time, he kept up.

When the mob finally reached safe ground behind the rocks, Joey stood there panting, exhilarated and grinning. Willa nuzzled him, her eyes shining with pride. Joey realized something wonderful: the very thing he had feared most — hopping on his own — had become the thing that saved him. And it happened only because he had refused to give up. Growing up, he understood now, meant facing your fears and practicing until you could stand — or hop — on your very own.
