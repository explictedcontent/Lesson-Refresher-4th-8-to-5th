🔢 Phase 2 — A Fraction OF a Whole Number (12 min)

Divide by the bottom, multiply by the top. Then prove it with the Group Splitter — 5 rounds.

📋 Divide by the Bottom, Multiply by the Top

To find a fraction of a whole number, the two numbers in the fraction each have a job:

1
The BOTTOM number says how many equal groups to break the whole thing into. So you divide by it.
2
The TOP number says how many of those groups you keep. So you multiply by it.
bottom → ÷   ·   top → ×
Worked example — 23 of 12
Bottom is 3 → break 12 into 3 equal groups12 ÷ 3 = 4, so each group holds 4
Top is 2 → keep 2 of those groups4 × 2 = 8
23 of 12 = 8
Check: the other group left over holds 4, and 8 + 4 = 12 ✓the groups always add back up to the whole
Worked example — 34 of 20
Bottom is 420 ÷ 4 = 5 in each group
Top is 35 × 3 = 15
34 of 20 = 15
Do it in that order and the numbers stay small. You could also multiply first (20 × 3 = 60) and then divide (60 ÷ 4 = 15) — you get the same 15. But dividing first keeps you working with 5s instead of 60s, and small numbers mean fewer mistakes.
Sanity check every single time. A fraction of something is only a piece of it, so your answer must be smaller than the number you started with (unless the top and bottom are equal). If you ask for 23 of 12 and get 18, you have gone the wrong way.

▦ Phase 3 — Fraction × Fraction: The Area Model (15 min)

This picture is the heart of the whole lesson. Build it yourself, then hit 6 targets.

📋 Why "Multiply the Tops, Multiply the Bottoms" Actually Works

Take a rectangle and cut it one way for the first fraction. Then cut the same rectangle the other way for the second fraction. The little boxes that got shaded BOTH ways are your answer.

Worked example — 12 × 13
Cut into 3 columns, shade 1that is one third of the rectangle, going up-and-down
Cut into 2 rows, shade 1that is one half of the rectangle, going across
Now count the little boxes: 2 rows × 3 columns = 6 boxes in the whole rectanglethe BOTTOMS multiplied: 2 × 3 = 6
Only 1 box is shaded both waysthe TOPS multiplied: 1 × 1 = 1
12 × 13 = 16
top × top  over  bottom × bottom
No common denominator needed — and that surprises people. When you ADD fractions you have to make the bottoms match first. When you MULTIPLY them you do not. Just go straight across: tops with tops, bottoms with bottoms. Multiplying is the easier one.
Worked example — 23 × 34
Tops: 2 × 3 = 6
Bottoms: 3 × 4 = 12
So far: 612correct, but not finished — it can be simplified
= 12  (divide top and bottom by 6)

🤯 Phase 4 — Why It Gets SMALLER, and Simplifying (12 min)

The part that trips up almost every 5th grader, plus tidying up your answer. Then 6 practice questions.

📋 The Surprising Part: Multiplying Can Make Things SMALLER

For years, multiplying meant "make it bigger." 8 × 3 = 24. 8 × 5 = 40. Bigger, bigger, bigger. Then fractions show up and break the pattern:

8 × 12 = 4

Four is smaller than eight. That is not a mistake.

Here is plainly why. Multiplying by a whole number means "make copies." 8 × 3 means three whole copies of 8, so of course it grows. But multiplying by 12 means "take a PART of it" — half of one single 8. You are not making copies, you are cutting a piece off. Taking a piece of something always leaves you with less than you started with.

MULTIPLIER BIGGER THAN 1

  • 8 × 3 = 24
  • 8 × 2 = 16
  • You make whole copies
  • Answer GROWS

MULTIPLIER LESS THAN 1

  • 8 × 34 = 6
  • 8 × 12 = 4
  • You take a piece
  • Answer SHRINKS
The in-between case. 8 × 1 = 8 — no change at all, because one whole copy is just the thing itself. So the full rule is: multiply by more than 1 → answer grows. Multiply by exactly 1 → answer stays. Multiply by less than 1 → answer shrinks. You can predict which way it goes before you calculate, which makes it a great way to catch your own mistakes.
⚠️ Two fractions less than 1 make an even tinier answer. 12 × 13 = 16, and a sixth is smaller than BOTH a half and a third. Look back at the area model: you took a third, and then you only kept half of that. Cutting a piece off a piece leaves something very small.

📋 Simplifying: Finishing the Job

Multiplying straight across often leaves a fraction that can be written in a smaller, tidier way. To simplify, divide the top and the bottom by the same number, and keep going until nothing but 1 divides into both.

Worked example — simplify 6/12
Both 6 and 12 divide by 6
6 ÷ 6 = 1  and  12 ÷ 6 = 2
612 = 12
Slower route: ÷2 gives 3/6, then ÷3 gives 1/2 — same place, more steps ✓small steps are fine, they just take longer
Worked example — simplify 8/12
Biggest number dividing into both 8 and 12 is 4
8 ÷ 4 = 2  and  12 ÷ 4 = 3
812 = 23
Only 1 divides into both 2 and 3 now, so it is fully simplifiedthat is what "lowest terms" means

📋 Whole Number × Fraction

A whole number is secretly a fraction — it is just sitting over a 1. So 6 is 61, and you can multiply straight across like always.

Worked example — 6 × 13
Write 6 as 61
Tops: 6 × 1 = 6  ·  Bottoms: 1 × 3 = 3
63 means 6 ÷ 3a fraction bar is a division sign
6 × 13 = 2
Same answer the Phase 2 way: 6 ÷ 3 = 2, then 2 × 1 = 2 ✓
Not every answer lands on a whole number. 4 × 23 = 83, and 8 ÷ 3 = 2 with 2 left over, so that is 2 and 23. Notice it is still smaller than 4 — two thirds of each of the 4 pieces, so less than 4. Today's practice sticks to the ones that come out clean, but do not be alarmed when a leftover shows up.
