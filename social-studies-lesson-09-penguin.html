Core Reading: The Continent at the Bottom of the World

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

At the very bottom of the world sits Antarctica, the fifth-largest continent. Almost all of it — nearly the whole continent — is buried under a thick ice sheet. That ice is so huge that it holds about 70% of all the fresh water on Earth. If you could stand there, you would see white in every direction.

Antarctica is a land of records. It is the coldest place on Earth — the lowest temperature ever measured there was about -128°F (-89°C). It is also the windiest continent. And here is a surprise: even though it is covered in ice, Antarctica is technically a desert. So little snow or rain falls that it counts as the driest continent on the planet.

Continent: one of Earth's seven great land masses.   Treaty: an official agreement between countries.   Research station: a base where scientists live and work.   Expedition: a long journey made for exploring or discovery.   Ice sheet: a giant thick layer of ice covering land.

Here is something amazing: no country owns Antarctica. In 1959, twelve countries signed the Antarctic Treaty. Today more than 50 countries have joined. The treaty sets the whole continent aside for peaceful science only — no military bases and no mining are allowed. It is shared by everyone.

Because it is so harsh, Antarctica has no permanent human residents. The only people there are scientists who rotate through research stations, such as McMurdo (run by the USA) and Amundsen-Scott, which sits right at the South Pole. They study the ice, the weather, and the sky, then go home and new scientists take their place.

Long before the scientists came the explorers. In 1911, Roald Amundsen of Norway became the first person to reach the South Pole. Robert Falcon Scott of Britain reached it just weeks later but tragically died on the journey home. And Ernest Shackleton's crew survived an incredible ordeal when their ship, the Endurance, was crushed by the ice — every crew member made it home alive.

Life in Antarctica clusters at the coasts and in the Southern Ocean that surrounds it: emperor penguins, seals, whales, and tiny shrimp-like krill that feed almost everything. Why does Antarctica matter today? Its ancient ice tells scientists about Earth's past climate, and when that ice melts, it raises sea levels all over the world. This frozen, empty continent affects every one of us.
