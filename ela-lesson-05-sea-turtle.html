🌙 Night Navigation Tap to reveal ▾

Baby sea turtles find the ocean at night by crawling toward the BRIGHTEST light on the horizon. Naturally, that's the moon's reflection on the sea — but artificial lights from buildings can dangerously confuse them!

🏖️ Born to Return Tap to reveal ▾

A female sea turtle returns to the EXACT beach where she was born — possibly 30 years later — to lay her own eggs. Scientists believe she uses Earth's magnetic field as a map!

😢 Not Really Crying Tap to reveal ▾

Sea turtles don't actually cry — those big "tears" you see when a female is nesting are from salt glands near their eyes that flush out excess ocean salt. It's like a built-in water filter!

Reading Passage
10 min

The Long Journey Home

Luna's world was warm, dark, and perfectly still — just a smooth oval shell pressed snug against eighty brothers and sisters beneath two feet of soft, sun-heated sand. She had been sleeping like that for almost two months, curled inside her egg on a Florida beach, waiting.

Then one night, something changed. A hatchling beside her tapped against its shell. Then another. All at once, as if a silent alarm had rung, the whole clutch cracked open together. Eighty tiny turtles — each no bigger than a cookie — scrambled upward through the sand in a squirming, kicking team. Safety in numbers: even if a ghost crab snatched one or two, most of the hatchlings would make it.

Luna burst through the surface and felt cool night air for the first time. The beach was silver with moonlight. Instinct — not thought, not memory, not anyone's instructions — told her exactly what to do. She turned toward the brightest glow on the horizon: the moon shimmering on the Atlantic Ocean, and she ran.

The first wave hit her like a wall and rolled her backward up the sand. She scrambled again. The second wave knocked her sideways. On the third try, she clawed forward with her tiny flippers, dove under the white foam, and suddenly the ocean took her in. The spinning and tumbling stopped. She was weightless. She was home.

For the next several years, Luna drifted in the great ocean current called the North Atlantic Gyre — a slow, spinning river of warm water that circled the ocean like a cradle. Scientists call this time the "lost years" because for decades, no one knew where young sea turtles went. Luna ate jellyfish, dodged sharks, and grew. Her small, soft shell hardened into a beautiful carapace, olive-green and smooth, shaped like the blade of an oar.

Not every danger wore fins. Sometimes Luna surfaced and found herself tangled in the mesh of a fishing net — not meant for her, but deadly all the same. She was lucky; she found a gap and escaped. Many sea turtles are not so fortunate. This is part of why sea turtles are endangered today.

Thirty years passed. Luna was now a full-grown loggerhead, five feet long and four hundred pounds. Then one morning, deep in the middle of the Atlantic, something tugged at a feeling she had never noticed before — a quiet pull in her brain, like a compass needle swinging north. It was navigation by Earth's magnetic field. Every beach on Earth has a unique magnetic "address," and Luna's brain had been storing hers since before she hatched. The signal said: go west. Go home.

Her migration across the Atlantic took months. She swam past cargo ships and coral reefs, under hurricanes and starry skies. One October night, her flippers finally touched warm, familiar sand. She crawled up the same beach where she had hatched. She dug a deep hole with her back flippers, laid over a hundred eggs, covered them gently, and slipped back into the surf.

Somewhere beneath the sand, eighty small heartbeats began to tick. The cycle had begun again.

Comprehension Check
8 min

Tap each question to reveal the answer. Think about your answer first!

1. Why do hatchlings emerge from the nest all at once? ▶
Safety in numbers — many hatchlings at once means predators can't eat them all, so more survive. The chaos of eighty turtles gives each individual a better chance.
2. How does Luna find her way back to her birth beach 30 years later? ▶
Sea turtles have magnetic sensing cells that detect Earth's magnetic field. Each beach has a unique magnetic "address," and Luna's brain stored that address before she even hatched.
3. What does the word migration mean in the passage? ▶
Migration means traveling a long distance from one place to another, usually to feed or to breed. Luna's migration was her long swim across the Atlantic Ocean to return home.
4. What human-made danger does Luna face during her ocean years? ▶
Fishing nets — Luna gets tangled in nets that were meant to catch fish, not turtles. This is called "bycatch." She escapes, but many sea turtles are not so lucky, which is one reason they are endangered.
5. Why is Luna's journey called a "cycle"? ▶
Because she hatched on that beach, traveled the ocean for decades, and returned to the exact same beach to lay her own eggs — completing a loop that repeats generation after generation. The last line of the passage ("The cycle had begun again") signals this.
