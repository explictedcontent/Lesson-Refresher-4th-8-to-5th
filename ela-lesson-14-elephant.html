Reading Passage
10 min

Tembo and the Long Memory

Tembo was a young elephant calf in a herd led by his grandmother, the old matriarch named Nyra. For as long as Tembo could remember, the plain had been green and the waterhole had always been full. But this year, a harsh dry season crept in. The grass turned brown and brittle, and the nearby waterhole shrank to a circle of cracked mud. The whole herd grew anxious and thirsty, swaying on their feet beneath the burning sun.

The other elephants turned and looked to Nyra. She was calm. Her wrinkled eyes seemed to hold a hundred dry seasons at once. "I remember a waterhole far from here," she said quietly, "from a drought long, long ago, when I was small. The water was there then. It will be there now."

Tembo was doubtful. He looked out across the shimmering plain, where the heat blurred the land into waves. The journey looked daunting and endless. "But Grandmother," he said, "what if the water is gone? What if you misremember?"

Nyra rested her trunk softly on his back. "The land does not change as fast as you fear," she told him. "Trust my memory. Follow, and be patient."

So the herd began to walk. They walked for two long days across the baking plain. Tembo's legs ached and his throat felt like sand, and more than once he wanted to stop and give up. But the older elephants would not let him fall behind. They encouraged him with low rumbles, and the tallest ones stepped in close to shield him from the worst of the sun.

Just when Tembo was certain he could not take another step, the herd crested a low hill — and there below them stretched a wide, glistening pool of water, exactly where Nyra had said it would be. The herd hurried down, and Tembo plunged his trunk in and drank deeply, the cool water spreading through him like life itself.

As he drank, Tembo understood something. The herd had survived only because of Nyra's wisdom and her long memory. That very day, he made a promise to himself: he would watch, and learn the route by heart, so that one day he could lead the herd too. Memory, he realized, was a kind of treasure — quietly passed down from the old to the young, keeping everyone alive.
