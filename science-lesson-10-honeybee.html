The Waggle Dance and Other Superpowers

From Egg to Adult

Every honeybee starts as a tiny egg. The egg hatches into a wriggly, wormlike larva that eats and grows. Then it becomes a resting pupa inside a sealed cell, where its body completely rebuilds. Finally, a full-grown adult bee chews its way out. This four-stage change — egg, larva, pupa, adult — is called complete metamorphosis, the very same life cycle a butterfly goes through.

The Waggle Dance

When a forager bee finds a great patch of flowers, she flies home and tells everyone — by dancing. She runs in a figure-8 and shakes her body in the middle. The angle of her waggle shows the direction to the flowers compared to the sun, and the length of her waggle shows how far away they are. It is a real map, danced in the dark hive, with no words at all.

Pollen Power

As a bee gathers sweet nectar, yellow pollen sticks to her fuzzy body. She carries it flower to flower, and this pollination fertilizes the plants so they can make fruit and seeds. To bring pollen home, she packs it into balls held by stiff hairs on her back legs called pollen baskets.

Making Honey

Bees suck up nectar and store it in a special honey stomach. Back at the hive, they bring it up and fan it with their wings to dry it into thick, sticky honey. Then they seal each cell with wax to save the food for winter. The honeycomb cells are all hexagons — six-sided shapes that store the most honey using the least wax. Bees are natural engineers!

Super Senses

A honeybee has five eyes — two big compound eyes and three tiny ones on top of her head. She can even see ultraviolet patterns on flowers that humans cannot see, like glowing landing lights. That buzzing sound? It is the noise of her wings beating over 200 times per second.

One Big Team

Bees are insects, so they are cold-blooded — they do not make steady body heat like we do. But together the whole hive acts like one giant "superorganism." In winter they cluster tightly and shiver their wing muscles to make heat, keeping the colony warm. Teamwork and communication turn thousands of tiny bees into a single, powerful family.
