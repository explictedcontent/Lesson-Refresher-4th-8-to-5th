🦑 The Monster Is a Real Animal Tap to reveal ▾

For hundreds of years sailors told stories about the kraken, a creature big enough to pull a ship under. Then in 1857 a Danish scientist named Japetus Steenstrup used beaks and body parts washed ashore to show the world what was really out there: the giant squid. The monster was an animal the whole time — we just had never gotten a good look at it.

👁️ Eyes the Size of Dinner Plates Tap to reveal ▾

A giant squid has the largest eyes of any animal on Earth — around 10 inches across, about the width of a dinner plate. Down where it lives there is almost no sunlight, so those enormous eyes are for spotting the faint glow of other animals, and for seeing a sperm whale coming before it arrives.

📹 Nobody Filmed One Alive Until 2012 Tap to reveal ▾

People had seen dead giant squid for over a century, but no one had ever filmed a living one in the deep ocean until 2012, when a team off Japan finally caught one on camera. Think about that: for most of history, everything anybody "knew" about this animal came from washed-up bodies and scared guesses.

🕸️ Lost Fishing Nets Keep Fishing Tap to reveal ▾

Fishing gear that gets lost overboard doesn't stop working. Divers call it ghost gear, and it drifts for years, tangling turtles, seals, dolphins, sharks, and squid. Rescue crews around the world do nothing but cut animals loose from it. A lot of "sea monster attacks" turn out to be an animal that is stuck and panicking.

The Thing Under the Silver Marlin

Part 1 — Eleven Nights of Stories

Wren Alcott had been a deckhand on the sailing ship Silver Marlin for eleven days, and in that time she had heard the story of the Deepmaw six times.

It got bigger every time.

On Monday the Deepmaw had arms as long as the mainmast. By Thursday the arms were as long as the whole ship. By Saturday night, with the cook, Nan Bly, leaning in close over her mug, the Deepmaw was the size of an island — a thing that lay waiting under the waves for a ship to sail across it, closed around the hull like a fist closing on a beetle, and pulled it down without a sound.

"And not one sailor ever came home to tell about it," Nan finished, the way she always did.

Wren was twelve, and she was not stupid. If nobody ever came home, she wanted to ask, then who told you? But every grown sailor at that table reached into a pocket and touched an iron nail when the Deepmaw came up — an old superstition against bad luck — and nobody was laughing, so Wren kept the question in her mouth.

Only Teodor Vask ever gave her a straight answer. He was the bosun, the oldest man aboard, and he had a habit of saying odd things while coiling rope.

"Each time that story goes around the table it grows an arm," he said. "Sailors exaggerate, girl. Not because they lie. Because there's a hole in the middle of that story where the truth ought to be, and fear moves into empty places."

Part 2 — The Water Goes Wrong

On the fourteenth night the wind died completely.

The sea went flat and black and glassy, and the Silver Marlin sat on it like a toy on a tabletop. Wren had the middle watch. She was counting stars and losing count when the deck jolted under her boots hard enough to sit her down.

Then the water off the starboard rail lifted.

It rose in a smooth dark hill, wider than the ship was long, and something inside that hill was pale. An immense shape slid up out of the dark — an arm, thicker than a barrel, longer than the mast, streaked with white — and slapped down across the rail six feet from where Wren sat.

Somebody screamed. A lantern went over. Boots came pounding up from below, and Nan Bly was shouting one word over and over: Deepmaw, Deepmaw, Deepmaw.

Part 3 — Wren Looks

Here is what Wren could never explain afterward: she did not run.

Everyone else went backward. Wren went forward, on her knees, dragging the lantern with her, until she was close enough to the rail to see.

The arm was not grabbing the ship. It was writhing — twisting, yanking, pulling in one direction over and over, the way you pull your foot when your shoelace is caught in a bike chain. Faint blue-green sparks ran down its skin in waves, bioluminescent light flickering on and off like breath.

Wren lifted the lantern higher, and she saw the net.

A great gray snarl of it, hundreds of feet of lost fishing net and rope and floats, wound around the arm and the body beneath. The animal was completely entangled — and the far end of that net was hooked hard around the Silver Marlin's anchor chain.

It wasn't dragging the ship down. It was stuck to the ship, and terrified, and trying to get away.

Then the water turned again, and an eye came up out of it. An eye as wide as a dinner plate, gold-rimmed, with a black slot down the middle of it. It rolled toward the lantern and it stopped, and it looked at Wren.

She had expected to see hate in it. There wasn't any. There was just an animal that wanted the net off.

Part 4 — The Knife

"Harpoons!" someone yelled. "Get the harpoons up!"

"NO." Wren was on her feet without deciding to be. "It isn't holding us! We're holding it — look at the anchor chain!"

Captain Orla Fenn shoved through the crowd, took one long look over the rail, and put a hand flat on Wren's shoulder. "Vask," she said. "Chair and lines. Nobody throws anything."

They rigged a bosun's chair off the rail. Wren went down it with a knife on a lanyard — she was the smallest and the net was tight — and Vask came after her on a second line.

Every cut had to be cautious, because an arm that could crush a rowboat was ten inches from her elbow. Wren talked the whole time, low and steady, mostly nonsense — almost, almost, hold still — the way you talk to a dog with a thorn in its paw.

The animal held still.

The last strand went at half past three in the morning. The huge arm slid off the rail, unhurried, not even brushing her, and the whole immense shape sank backward into the black — and just before it disappeared it lit up, top to bottom, one long pulse of blue-green light.

Part 5 — The Hole in the Story

Nobody on deck said anything for a while.

"What did it look like?" Nan Bly finally asked. "Up close. What did it really look like?"

Wren thought about it. "Scared," she said.

Vask started coiling the cut net onto the deck, because that was Vask. "Fourteen nights you listened to us make that thing the size of an island," he said. "Took you about fourteen seconds with a lantern to find out what it actually was."

Wren spent the rest of that voyage filling a notebook. She drew the arm and marked its width against a barrel. She wrote down the shape of the eye, the color of the light, how the arm moved when it was stuck and how it moved when it was free. At the back she kept a list of the questions she still could not answer, because Vask said the list of what you don't know is the most honest page in any book.

The crew still told the Deepmaw story. Sailors always will. But now, when it reached the part about the island-sized monster that hates ships, Wren would slide her notebook down the table, open it, and say, "Want to see what it really looks like?"

And the hole in the middle of the story got a little smaller every time.

1. How does the Deepmaw story change each time the crew tells it? ▶
It gets bigger. On Monday the arms are as long as the mainmast, by Thursday as long as the whole ship, and by Saturday the creature is the size of an island. Vask says the story "grows an arm" every time it goes around the table.
2. What question does Wren want to ask about the story, and why doesn't she? ▶
If no sailor who saw the Deepmaw ever came home, then who told the story in the first place? She keeps quiet because the grown sailors touch iron nails for luck when the Deepmaw comes up, and nobody is laughing — she is the new deckhand and she doesn't want to be the one who laughs.
3. What does Wren see when she brings the lantern close that everyone else missed? ▶
She sees that the arm is writhing, not grabbing, and that the animal is wrapped in a huge snarl of lost fishing net — and that the net is hooked around the ship's own anchor chain. The creature isn't attacking the ship; it is stuck to it and trying to get away.
4. Why does Captain Fenn say "Nobody throws anything"? ▶
Because Wren has just given her new information — the animal is caught, not attacking — and the captain looks for herself before deciding. A harpoon would hurt an animal that is already trapped, and it would not free the ship. Cutting the net solves the real problem.
5. What is the THEME (big idea) of the story? ▶
Fear grows in the gaps where knowledge is missing. Vask says it out loud: "fear moves into empty places." The crew filled fourteen nights of not-knowing with a monster; Wren filled the same gap with a lantern, a notebook, and a careful look — and the monster turned out to be a frightened animal in a net.

🔍 What Does "Compare & Contrast" Mean?

Two words, two jobs:

🤝 COMPARE = tell how two things are ALIKE.
✂️ CONTRAST = tell how two things are DIFFERENT.

Easy memory trick: con-TRAST has a T in it, like Telling them apart.

Signal words tell you which one is happening. Authors leave these lying around like footprints:

🤝 ALIKE signals: both · also · too · similarly · alike · just like · in the same way · as well as

✂️ DIFFERENT signals: but · however · unlike · while · instead · on the other hand · different from · although

The tool: a three-column chart. Left = only the first thing. Right = only the second thing. Middle = BOTH.

ONLY A SHARK
breathes underwater through gills · lays eggs or gives birth and swims off · rows of replaceable teeth
BOTH
live in the ocean · have fins and smooth bodies · are strong swimmers · hunt fish
ONLY A DOLPHIN
a mammal — must surface to breathe air · feeds its babies milk · uses clicks to find food

Now turn the chart into sentences, using signal words:

🤝 "Both sharks and dolphins are fast ocean hunters with fins."
✂️ "A shark breathes underwater through gills; a dolphin, however, has to come up for air."

Two traps to dodge:

🪤 The "it's in this paragraph so it's only true here" trap. Check BOTH sides before you say "only." Lots of details belong in the middle.
🪤 The "true but not shared" trap. If only one side says it, it does not go in the middle — no matter how much you'd like it to.

Golden rule: point at the exact words in the text that put it where you put it.
