The Kraken Was a Guess. The Squid Is the Evidence.

A Monster in the Logbooks

For hundreds of years, sailors in the cold seas off Norway and Iceland came home with the same story. Somewhere out past the last island there lived a thing called the kraken — a creature so vast that sailors mistook its back for an island, with arms that could reach a ship's mast and pull the whole vessel under. In the 1700s a Norwegian bishop named Erik Pontoppidan even wrote the kraken up in a natural history book as if it were a real, catalogued animal.

Let's be honest right away: the kraken is not real. No animal on Earth is the size of an island, and no squid has ever been shown to drag a ship under.

But here is the strange part, and it is the reason this lesson exists. The sailors were not making it up out of nothing. Sometimes a body washed ashore — a pale, torn thing with ten limbs and a beak like a parrot's, longer than a rowboat. Those bodies were real. They belonged to an animal that turned out to be weirder than the monster people invented for it.

Meet the Real Giants

The giant squid, scientific name Architeuthis, can reach about 13 meters (43 feet) from the tip of its tail to the tips of its two long feeding tentacles. Most of that length is those two stretchy tentacles, so it is not 43 feet of solid animal — but a big female can still weigh a couple hundred kilograms.

Then there is the colossal squid, Mesonychoteuthis, which lives in the freezing water around Antarctica. It is not as long overall, but it is heavier and much bulkier, with a thicker body — and instead of only suckers, it carries sharp swiveling hooks on its arms. The colossal squid is the heaviest invertebrate (animal without a backbone) known to science.

Both are cephalopods — the group of mollusks that also includes octopuses, cuttlefish, and nautiluses. A quick check you can use forever: a squid has eight arms plus two longer feeding tentacles, ten limbs in all. An octopus has eight arms and no tentacles.

The Eye the Size of a Dinner Plate

Here is the fact that stops people. A giant squid's eye measures about 25–27 centimeters (roughly 10 inches) across. That is the largest eye of any known animal — bigger than a basketball player's hand, about the width of a dinner plate. A human eye is about 2.4 cm.

Why build something that enormous? Because of where the squid lives. Deep down, sunlight is gone and the only light is the faint flicker that living things make themselves. A gigantic eye gathers more of that tiny light, the way a bigger bucket catches more rain. Scientists think it may let a giant squid notice the glow stirred up by an approaching sperm whale — its main predator — from far enough away to run. It is not an eye for seeing detail. It is an eye for seeing almost nothing at all.

Down Through the Zones

Scientists split the ocean into layers by how much sunlight reaches them.

The sunlight zone runs from the surface down to about 200 meters. There is enough light here for photosynthesis, so this is where nearly all the ocean's plants and algae live, and where most of the fish you can name swim.

The twilight zone runs from about 200 m down to about 1,000 m. A dim blue haze filters down — enough to see by, but far too little for a plant to live on. This is the layer where bioluminescence starts to take over as the main source of light.

The midnight zone begins around 1,000 m and runs down toward the abyss. Sunlight is completely gone. It is permanently black, near freezing, and the pressure is crushing — roughly one extra atmosphere of pressure for every 10 meters you go down, so at 1,000 m the water is pressing about 100 times harder than the air does at the beach. Giant squid are usually found in the twilight and midnight zones.

Notice the pattern going down: light disappears, pressure increases, temperature drops.

Living Light

Bioluminescence is light an animal makes inside its own body with a chemical reaction. A chemical called luciferin reacts with oxygen, helped along by a protein, and the energy comes out as light instead of as much heat — which is why it is sometimes called "cold light." It is astonishingly common down there; most animals in the deep open ocean can do it.

What do they use it for? Three big jobs. Some animals lure prey with a glowing tip, like the anglerfish's lit-up fishing rod. Some confuse predators — a few deep-sea squid squirt a cloud of glowing mucus instead of black ink, since a black cloud is useless in a place that is already black. And some signal to their own kind, flashing patterns to find a mate or a group.

The Squid's Toolkit

A squid's body is a set of solutions to hard problems.

Jet propulsion. A squid pulls water into its mantle — the muscular sac that makes up most of its body — then squeezes and forces that water out through a narrow nozzle called the siphon. Push water one way, the squid shoots the other way. The siphon can be aimed, so a squid can jet backward, forward, or sideways.

Ink. Squid store dark ink and release it when threatened. Sometimes it spreads into a smokescreen; sometimes it holds together as a blob roughly the squid's own size and shape — a decoy for a predator to grab while the real squid jets away.

Chromatophores. Squid skin is packed with these: tiny sacs of pigment with muscles attached. Pull the muscles and the sac stretches wide, showing its color; relax them and it shrinks to a dot. That is how a squid changes color in under a second, for camouflage or for signaling.

The beak. A squid has no bones anywhere, but it does have a hard, sharp beak made of a tough material called chitin, shaped a lot like a parrot's. It is the toughest part of the animal, which matters for a reason we are about to get to.

Three hearts and blue blood. A squid has three hearts: two branchial hearts that pump blood through the gills to load it with oxygen, and one systemic heart that pumps it to the rest of the body. And the blood is blue, not red. Our red blood carries oxygen using hemoglobin, which is built around iron. A squid uses hemocyanin, built around copper — and copper-based blood turns blue when it picks up oxygen. Hemocyanin does a better job than our blood in water that is very cold and low in oxygen, which is exactly the water a giant squid lives in. The extra hearts help push that thick blue blood through a big body in the cold.

The Whale That Hunts Them

The giant squid's great enemy is the sperm whale, which dives regularly past 1,000 meters — and sometimes far deeper — holding its breath for well over an hour, hunting squid in total darkness using echolocation (sending out clicks and listening for the echoes).

And the whales left scientists evidence. Sperm whales are sometimes found with rings of round sucker scars on their skin, and squid beaks pile up inside their stomachs, because the beak is too tough to digest. Researchers could count those beaks and measure those scars long before anyone had seen a living giant squid at all. The animal was proven by the marks it left on its predator.

How Science Actually Learned This

Look at the order things happened, because it is a perfect example of how science works.

First: carcasses. For centuries the only giant squid anyone examined were dead ones — washed up on a beach or hauled up torn in a fishing net. You can measure a body, but a dead animal cannot show you how it swims, hunts, or behaves.

Then: stomach contents. Beaks from sperm whale stomachs told researchers which squid species the whales ate, roughly how big they were, and how many. Indirect evidence, but real evidence.

Then: cameras. In 2004, Japanese scientists Tsunemi Kubodera and Kyoichi Mori got the first photographs of a living giant squid attacking a baited line in the deep. And in 2012, a team finally filmed a live giant squid in its own deep-sea habitat — the first video of the real animal, alive, where it lives. People had been telling kraken stories for roughly 300 years before anyone got that footage.

That is the honest shape of science. The evidence got better; the story got corrected. A scientist saying "we don't know yet" is not a weakness — it is the sentence that keeps the search going.

🔎 Myth check: Giant squid do not attack ships, hunt swimmers, or grow to the size of an island. They live far offshore and mostly very deep, and they are hunted by whales far more than they hunt anything of ours. When you meet a monster story, ask the scientist's question: what is the evidence, and how good is it?
