Wolves of the Northern World

Read carefully — the quiz and activities come from this passage.

High in a snowy forest, a howl rolls across the hills. It is a gray wolf calling to its family. Gray wolves are among the most far-traveled land animals on Earth. They live all across the top half of our planet, called the Northern Hemisphere. You can find wild wolves on three different continents: North America, Europe, and Asia. From the deep woods of Canada to the mountains of Italy to the cold forests of Russia, wolves have learned to survive in many kinds of places.

Wolves are not picky about their home, called their habitat. They live in thick forests, on the wide open tundra near the Arctic, and up in rocky mountains. As long as there is space to roam and prey to hunt, a wolf pack can make a living. A pack is a wolf family that hunts and travels together, usually led by a mother and father wolf.

Habitat: the natural home where an animal lives.   Hemisphere: one half of the Earth — northern or southern.   Continent: one of Earth's seven huge landmasses.   National park: land the government protects for nature and people.   Conservation: protecting animals and wild places so they last.

One of the most famous wolf homes in the world is Yellowstone National Park. It sits in the western United States and stretches across three states: Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho. Yellowstone became the very first national park in the world, way back in 1872. A national park is a place the government sets aside so that its land, plants, and animals stay protected for everyone to enjoy.

People have told stories about wolves for thousands of years. Many Native American nations honor the wolf in their traditions as a symbol of loyalty, family, and working together — because wolves take such good care of their packs. Far away in Europe, an ancient Roman legend says that two baby brothers named Romulus and Remus were saved and raised by a mother wolf, and that Romulus later founded the city of Rome. Cultures across Europe and Asia have their own wolf tales too. Many different cultures tell stories about wolves, and the stories are not all the same.

Wolves and people have not always gotten along. Long ago, many wolves in the United States were hunted and removed, and by about 1926 there were no wolves left in Yellowstone at all. Without wolves, the park changed: too many elk ate too many young trees. Then people made a new choice. In 1995, scientists carefully brought wolves back to Yellowstone from Canada. This is called conservation — protecting nature so it can thrive. Laws like the Endangered Species Act and the work of national parks helped the wolves return.

Bringing wolves back shows how communities make choices about the natural world. People can decide to protect an animal, pass laws to help it, and set aside parks where it is safe. Today wolves once again howl across Yellowstone — a real place on the map, and a real story about how people and nature share the Earth.
